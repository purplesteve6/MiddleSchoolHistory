October–December 1859

Trial, Execution & Legacy

Captured alive at Harpers Ferry, Brown soon became more powerful as a symbol than he had ever been as a military leader.

Brown's execution did not end the argument over him. It transformed him into a symbol that different Americans interpreted in completely different ways.

The Trial

Brown was taken to nearby Charles Town, Virginia. His trial began on October 27, 1859, only days after his capture. He faced charges of treason against Virginia, murder, and conspiring with enslaved people to rebel.

Brown was badly wounded and sometimes lay on a cot during the proceedings. The jury found him guilty on all charges. On November 2 he was sentenced to death.

“I feel no consciousness of guilt.”
From Brown's statement to the court before sentencing, November 2, 1859. His full statement argued that he intended to free enslaved people and denied that his goal was indiscriminate violence.

From Prisoner to Symbol

Brown used his weeks in jail to write letters and defend his cause. Newspapers across the country reported his words. Many white Southerners saw him as proof that abolitionism could lead to armed attacks and slave rebellion. Many Northerners condemned the raid but admired Brown's courage at trial. Some abolitionists openly treated him as a martyr.

Future Lincoln assassin John Wilkes Booth joined a Virginia militia unit and witnessed Brown's execution.

December 2, 1859

Brown was hanged in Charles Town on December 2. Military forces guarded the execution site because officials feared an attempted rescue.

Shortly before his death, Brown handed a jailer a final written note. Its message was grim: he now believed the “crimes of this guilty land” would not be ended without bloodshed.

Less than a year and a half later, the Civil War began.

“...the crimes of this guilty land will never be purged away but with blood.”
John Brown's final written words, December 2, 1859. Excerpted for length.

Evidence: Brown dedicated much of his life to destroying slavery, helped enslaved people reach Canada, and risked his life in an attempt to free others.
Evidence: Brown remained defiant at trial and accepted execution rather than renounce his cause. His death inspired antislavery supporters and songs such as “John Brown's Body.”
Evidence: At Pottawatomie, Brown led a party that took five proslavery settlers from their homes and killed them. People also died during the Harpers Ferry raid.
Evidence: Brown planned an armed campaign against slavery, seized federal property, took hostages, and hoped to create a growing force that would destabilize slavery across the South.

Why His Legacy Matters

The Harpers Ferry raid intensified sectionalism. Southern fears of abolitionist violence grew. In the North, reactions ranged from condemnation to admiration.

Frederick Douglass later argued that although Brown failed to leave Harpers Ferry alive, his sacrifice helped strike a powerful blow against slavery. Brown's raid did not cause the Civil War by itself, but it became one of the dramatic events that pushed an already divided nation closer to war.
