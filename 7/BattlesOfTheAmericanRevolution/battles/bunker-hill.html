“A Costly Victory”

Bunker Hill

The Battle of Bunker Hill was one of the first big battles near Boston. The British captured the hill, but only after suffering huge casualties. The fight convinced both sides the war would be longer—and harder—than many expected.

A battle can be a “loss” on paper—but still boost confidence and change how the enemy thinks.

Battle at a Glance

🗓️ Date: June 17, 1775
📍 Place: Charlestown (near Boston), Massachusetts
👥 Who fought: British regulars vs. colonial militia
🎯 British goal: take the high ground and weaken the siege of Boston
🏆 Outcome: British capture the hill, but with heavy losses

Background

After Lexington and Concord, thousands of militia members surrounded Boston, beginning the siege of the city. The British still controlled Boston, but the Patriots controlled much of the land around it.

Both sides focused on the hills near the city. If the Patriots could place cannons on high ground, they could threaten British ships and troops. If the British controlled the hills, they could break out of Boston more easily. That struggle over high ground led directly to the Battle of Bunker Hill.

Most of the fighting happened on Breed’s Hill, not Bunker Hill—but the battle kept the name “Bunker Hill” anyway.

Quick Facts

⛰️ Patriots built defenses overnight on Breed’s Hill.
🚶 British troops attacked in uphill lines—multiple times.
💥 Patriots had limited ammunition and supplies.
🔥 Charlestown burned during the battle.
⚰️ British losses were shockingly high.

Vocabulary

fortification — a defensive structure built for protection.
redoubt — a small temporary fort, often made of dirt and wood.
casualties — soldiers killed or wounded.
assault — a strong attack on a position.
ammunition — bullets and gunpowder used for weapons.
retreat — to pull back from a fight.

What Happened?

Patriots dig in on the high ground

On the night of June 16, Patriot forces moved quietly onto the Charlestown peninsula. Under the cover of darkness, they built fortifications including a redoubt on Breed’s Hill. By morning, British leaders in Boston could clearly see the new defenses overlooking the city.

The British decide to attack

The British could not ignore an enemy position so close to Boston. They planned a direct assault: troops would cross the water, form lines, and march uphill to take the Patriot defenses. It was a bold plan—but it meant British soldiers would be exposed as they climbed.

Two attacks fail

As the first British lines moved uphill, Patriots waited until the soldiers were close enough to make their shots count. The British were driven back. The British tried again—and were driven back a second time. The hillside was crowded, loud, and dangerous, and British casualties quickly increased.

The third attack succeeds—because Patriots run out of ammunition

Patriots were brave, but they had a major weakness: not enough ammunition. On the third attack, many Patriot defenders could not fire effectively and were forced to retreat. The British finally captured the hill, but they paid a terrible price in losses.

Charlestown burns

During the fighting, Charlestown caught fire (partly from cannon and naval gunfire). Smoke filled the sky, turning the battle into a dramatic scene visible from Boston and the surrounding towns.

Why Did Bunker Hill Matter?

Bunker Hill mattered because it changed the way both sides understood the war. The British “won” the ground, but their losses were so high that leaders realized the colonists would not be easy to defeat. A victory that costs too much is sometimes called a Pyrrhic victory.

For the Patriots, the battle was a confidence boost. Militia forces had stood up to professional soldiers in a major fight. Even after retreating, many Patriots believed: “We can do this.” That belief helped keep the resistance going.
