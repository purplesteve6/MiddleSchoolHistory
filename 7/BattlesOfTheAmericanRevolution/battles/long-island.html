August 27, 1776 • New York
“The Battle for New York City”

Long Island

The Battle of Long Island (also called the Battle of Brooklyn) was the first major battle after independence was declared. The British used strong planning and a surprise flank to defeat Washington’s army. But the Americans escaped in a daring nighttime evacuation that kept the war going.

Sometimes the most important part of a battle is not winning—it’s surviving and learning.

Battle at a Glance

🗓️ Date: August 27, 1776
📍 Place: Long Island (Brooklyn), New York
👥 Who fought: British & Hessian forces vs. the Continental Army
🎯 British goal: capture New York City and crush the rebellion early
🏃 Outcome: British victory; Americans retreat and escape to Manhattan

Background

In the summer of 1776, the war shifted to New York. The British believed New York City was the perfect place to strike because it had a huge harbor, important trade routes, and many people who still supported Britain. The Americans, led by George Washington, also knew New York was important—but defending it was difficult because it was made up of islands and waterways.

The British had a powerful navy and a large army. Washington’s Continental Army had courage, but it was less experienced and often short on supplies. When the British landed troops on Long Island, a major showdown was almost unavoidable.

During the American escape from Brooklyn, thick fog and darkness helped hide the boats—many British soldiers had no idea the Continental Army was slipping away.

Quick Facts

🚢 Britain used its navy to control the harbor and rivers.
🧭 British forces used a major flanking move to surprise Americans.
🧱 Americans built defenses around Brooklyn Heights.
🌫️ A nighttime evacuation saved Washington’s army.
🏙️ The British later occupied New York City.

Vocabulary

Continental Army — the main American army during the Revolution.
Hessians — German soldiers hired by Britain to fight.
flank — to attack from the side of an enemy.
evacuation — a planned move away from danger to save people or an army.
harbor — a sheltered area of water where ships can anchor safely.
fortification — a defensive structure built for protection.

What Happened?

The British land on Long Island

British forces crossed from Staten Island and landed on Long Island in August 1776. They brought tens of thousands of troops—the largest British force sent to North America so far—including Hessians. Washington positioned many Americans behind hills and passes, hoping to slow down the British advance toward Brooklyn.

A surprise flanking attack

The Americans expected most fighting to happen at the main roads through the hills. But the British found a less-guarded route. In a major flank, British troops marched at night and attacked from an unexpected direction. Many American units were caught off guard, and the fighting turned into a hard retreat back toward the defenses at Brooklyn Heights.

Americans trapped in Brooklyn

After the battle, the Americans were crowded behind their fortifications near the East River. They were exhausted and low on supplies. The British had won the field and could prepare to attack the defenses. If Washington’s army was captured, the Revolution might end quickly—because the Continental Army was the main force holding the rebellion together.

The nighttime evacuation

Washington made a bold decision: escape across the East River to Manhattan. During the night of August 29–30, soldiers were quietly moved onto boats. The operation was risky—if the British discovered it, they could attack the crowded shoreline. But darkness and fog helped hide the evacuation. By morning, most of the Continental Army had crossed safely.

Why Did Long Island Matter?

Long Island mattered because it showed how powerful the British army and navy could be, especially in a place full of waterways. It also taught Washington important lessons about training, scouting, and defending large cities.

Most importantly, Washington’s evacuation saved the Continental Army. If that army had been captured in 1776, the Revolution could have ended early. Surviving Long Island allowed the Americans to keep fighting—and to regroup for the next battles in New York.
