Lexington & Concord

Lexington and Concord were the first major clashes of the American Revolution. What began as a British mission to seize supplies turned into a day of fighting that convinced many colonists the conflict had become a real war.

A small clash can start a big war—especially when trust is already broken and both sides expect the worst.

Background

By 1775, Boston and the surrounding towns were full of tension. Many colonists were angry about British taxes and laws, and some towns were storing weapons and gunpowder in case fighting broke out. Britain sent more troops—called regulars—to enforce order. Colonial leaders feared Britain might arrest Patriot leaders and take away supplies before the colonies could organize.

British officials decided to march from Boston to Concord to seize military supplies. But Patriots had a network of riders and messengers, ready to warn towns if soldiers were on the move. That night, the alarm spread quickly—and thousands of militia members began preparing to defend their communities.

The famous “midnight ride” is often linked to Paul Revere, but he wasn’t the only rider. Others—like William Dawes—also carried warnings to help spread the alarm.

What Happened?

1) The British march out of Boston

Late on April 18, British troops quietly marched from Boston toward Concord. Their mission was to seize supplies and stop the colonies from preparing for war. British leaders also hoped to prevent the militia from gathering.

2) Lexington: a tense standoff

At dawn on April 19, British troops reached Lexington. A small group of militia stood on the town green. No one knows exactly who fired the first shot—often called “the shot heard ’round the world”. But once shooting started, the standoff turned deadly. The militia scattered, and the British continued on toward Concord.

3) Concord: the fight spreads

In Concord, Patriots had already moved many supplies to safer places. Fighting broke out near the North Bridge when militia members advanced and exchanged fire with British soldiers. This time, many militia units held their ground, and the British began to feel pressure.

4) The long retreat back to Boston

As British troops marched back toward Boston, militia fighters from many towns attacked from behind walls, trees, and hills. The British column suffered heavy losses and struggled to stay organized. By the time the troops reached safety near Boston, it was clear that the conflict had grown beyond a single mission.

Why Did Lexington & Concord Matter?

Lexington and Concord mattered because they proved that colonial militia could organize quickly and fight back. The battles also showed that British troops were not invincible—especially when they were far from support and surrounded by hostile towns.

After April 19, thousands of militia members surrounded Boston, beginning the siege of the city. Many colonists who had hoped the crisis would end peacefully now believed war had already begun.

Turning Point Check: This battle didn’t “win” the war—but it changed the situation from protests to open fighting.
