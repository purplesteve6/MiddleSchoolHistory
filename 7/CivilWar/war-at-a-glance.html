1861–1865 • Four Years of War

War at a Glance

Once fighting began, both sides expected victory sooner than it actually came. The war expanded across thousands of miles, killed hundreds of thousands of people, destroyed communities, and transformed the meaning of the conflict.

The Union's original goal was preserving the United States. By 1863, destroying slavery had also become an official war aim.
4 YEARSFrom Fort Sumter to Appomattox

This was more than a list of battles.

Military victories mattered, but so did politics, enslaved people's actions, Black military service, elections, laws, resources, transportation, and public support.

What Was Each Side Fighting For?

Union

The United States fought first to stop secession and preserve the Union. As the war continued, Lincoln used wartime powers against slavery, and the destruction of slavery became part of the Union cause.

Confederacy

The Confederacy fought to win independence from the United States. Its leaders sought to protect a social and political system built around slavery and hoped to force the Union to accept Southern independence.

Advantages—and Problems

The Union had...

  • A much larger population
  • More factories and industrial capacity
  • More railroad mileage
  • A stronger navy and larger financial system
  • The ability to replace men and supplies more easily over a long war

The Confederacy had...

  • The advantage of fighting mostly on familiar ground
  • A vast territory the Union had to invade and hold
  • Strong early military leadership in the eastern theater
  • A strategy that did not require conquering the North—only surviving long enough to make the Union quit

How the War Changed

1861

War Begins

Fort Sumter and the First Battle of Bull Run shattered hopes for a quick, easy victory. Both governments built enormous volunteer armies.

1862

The War Gets Bloodier

Large battles such as Shiloh and Antietam showed how destructive modern weapons and mass armies could be. After Antietam, Lincoln announced the preliminary Emancipation Proclamation.

Jan. 1, 1863

Emancipation Changes the War

The Emancipation Proclamation declared enslaved people in areas still in rebellion to be free. It did not immediately free everyone, but it made emancipation an official Union war aim and opened the door to large-scale Black military service.

July 1863

Gettysburg & Vicksburg

Union victories in Pennsylvania and Mississippi marked a major turning point. Lee's army retreated from Gettysburg, while Vicksburg gave the Union control of the Mississippi River.

1864–1865

The Confederacy Is Squeezed

Union armies pushed deeper into the South, destroying railroads, supplies, and infrastructure. In January 1865, Congress passed the 13th Amendment and sent it to the states for ratification.

Apr. 9, 1865

Lee Surrenders

Robert E. Lee surrendered the Army of Northern Virginia to Ulysses S. Grant at Appomattox Court House. Other Confederate armies surrendered soon afterward.

Apr. 14, 1865

Lincoln Is Assassinated

Just days after Appomattox, John Wilkes Booth shot President Lincoln at Ford's Theatre. Lincoln died the next morning, changing the leadership of the nation at the very moment Reconstruction was beginning.

What Did Union Victory Change?

The Union SurvivedThe United States remained one country.
Slavery EndedThe 13th Amendment abolished slavery throughout the United States when it was ratified in December 1865.
A New Fight BeganThe country now faced Reconstruction: how to rebuild the South and define freedom, citizenship, and political rights.
