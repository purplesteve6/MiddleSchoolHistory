Key Battles of the Civil War

The Civil War was not one long fight. It was a chain of battles and campaigns shaped by geography, technology, politics, slavery, leadership, and resources. These pages follow the war from the first shots at Fort Sumter to the surrender at Appomattox, helping students see how the conflict changed over time.

Background: More Than a List of Battles

Why the war happened

The war began after slaveholding states seceded from the Union following Abraham Lincoln’s election. At the center of the crisis was slavery. Early in the war, the Union fought to preserve the United States. Over time, emancipation became a central Union war aim too.

How students should read battles

Do not look only for “who won.” Ask what a battle changed. Did it stop an invasion? Open a river? Reveal new technology? Lead to a change in generals? Affect an election? Battles connect military events to the larger story of the war.

Politics stayed in the room

Presidents watched generals, generals replaced one another, emancipation reshaped the war, and voters still went to the polls in 1864. Civil War battles were military events, but they always had political consequences too.

How the War Changed

1861Secession crisis becomes open war. Both sides still imagine a short conflict.
1862Casualties soar. Armies grow. The war expands across the East, the West, rivers, and the coast.
1862–1863Antietam and emancipation show that slavery and war are now tightly linked. Gettysburg and Vicksburg shift momentum.
1864–1865Grant applies attrition against Lee while Sherman uses hard war against the Confederacy’s ability to resist.
Flags in the Smoke

Wait—Which Confederate Flag?

The best-known Confederate flag today is not the Confederacy’s first national flag. On Civil War battlefields, flags helped soldiers identify units and hold formations together through smoke and confusion. That practical problem is why the Confederacy used several different flags—and why one battle flag design later became the most familiar symbol.

First National: “Stars and Bars”

Used nationally: 1861–1863. This is the actual Stars and Bars. Its red-white-red stripes and blue canton could look too much like the U.S. flag at a distance.

Army of Northern Virginia Battle Flag

Widely issued from 1862. The square red flag with a blue saltire was easier to recognize in battle and became especially famous in Lee’s army.

Second National: “Stainless Banner”

Used nationally: 1863–1865. It placed the battle-flag design in the canton of a mostly white national flag.

Third National Flag

Adopted March 1865. A red vertical bar was added near the end of the war, but the Confederacy collapsed soon afterward.

So why is this the flag everyone recognizes?

The familiar rectangular red flag with a blue X comes from Confederate battle-flag designs. The Army of Northern Virginia version was square, but rectangular versions became especially common after the war because they were easier to manufacture and display. During the 20th century, this design was also used by white segregationists resisting the civil-rights movement, which is one reason it remains controversial today.

That means students may hear very different descriptions of the same symbol: heritage, regional pride, military memory, slavery, segregation, or white supremacy. History helps explain why those meanings collide.
