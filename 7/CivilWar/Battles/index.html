Background: More Than a List of Battles

Why the war happened

The Civil War began after slaveholding states seceded from the Union following Abraham Lincoln’s election. At the center of the crisis was slavery. Early in the war, the Union fought to preserve the United States. Over time, emancipation became a central Union war aim too.

How to read a battle

Do not look only for who won the field. Ask what changed. Did the battle stop an invasion? Open a river? reveal a new technology? lead to a general being replaced? or help shape a political decision like emancipation or the election of 1864?

Politics never disappeared

Presidents watched generals, generals were replaced, voters still went to the polls, and military events influenced public opinion. Civil War battles were military events, but they were always tied to larger political choices.

Flags in the Smoke

Wait—Which Confederate Flag?

The best-known Confederate flag today is not the Confederacy’s first national flag. On Civil War battlefields, flags helped soldiers identify units and hold formations together through smoke and confusion. That practical need is one reason the Confederacy used several important flags—and why one battle-flag design later became the most familiar symbol.

First National: “Stars and Bars”

Used nationally: 1861–1863. This is the actual Stars and Bars. Its red-white-red stripes and blue canton could look too much like the U.S. flag at a distance.

Army of Northern Virginia Battle Flag

Widely issued from 1862. This square battle flag was easier to recognize through battlefield smoke and became especially famous in Lee’s army.

Second National: “Stainless Banner”

Used nationally: 1863–1865. It placed a version of the battle-flag design in the canton of a mostly white national flag.

Third National Flag

Adopted March 1865. A red vertical bar was added late in the war, but the Confederacy collapsed soon afterward.

So why is this the flag everyone recognizes?

The familiar rectangular red flag with a blue X comes from Confederate battle-flag designs. The Army of Northern Virginia version was square, but rectangular versions became especially common after the war because they were easier to manufacture and display. During the 20th century, this design was also used by white segregationists resisting the civil-rights movement, which is one reason it remains controversial today.

That is why students may hear very different descriptions of the same symbol: military memory, regional pride, slavery, segregation, or white supremacy. History helps explain why those meanings collide.
