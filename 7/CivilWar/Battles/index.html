Key Battles of the Civil War

The Civil War was not one long battle. It was fought across thousands of miles by huge armies, small detachments, gunboats, cavalry, and new ironclad warships. These twelve battles and campaigns show how the war changed—from early hopes for a quick victory to a grinding struggle over armies, resources, slavery, and the survival of the United States.

How to Read the War

Geography

Rivers, railroads, ports, ridges, roads, and cities could be more important than a single patch of ground.

Technology

Rifled weapons, railroads, telegraphs, steamships, and ironclads changed how armies and navies moved and fought.

Resources

By 1864, destroying an enemy’s ability to supply armies became just as important as winning a dramatic battlefield victory.

Politics

Antietam affected emancipation. Atlanta affected an election. Battlefield events could change what happened far from the fighting.

Flags in the Smoke

Wait—Which “Confederate Flag”?

Flags were military tools as well as symbols. Soldiers used them to identify units, keep formations together, and rally in the smoke and confusion of battle. That practical problem helps explain why the Confederacy ended up with several important flags—and why the one most familiar today was not its first national flag.

First National: “Stars and Bars”

Used nationally: 1861–1863. The original version had seven stars; more were added as the Confederacy claimed additional states. Its broad red-white-red stripes and blue canton could resemble the U.S. flag at a distance.

Army of Northern Virginia Battle Flag

Created in 1861; widely issued in 1862. The square red flag with the blue St. Andrew’s cross was designed to be easier to recognize on a smoky battlefield after confusion at First Bull Run.

Second National: “Stainless Banner”

Used nationally: 1863–1865. It placed a version of the battle flag in the canton of a mostly white field. When hanging limp, however, it could look like a white flag of surrender.

Third National Flag

Adopted March 1865. A red vertical bar was added to the white field. It was official for only a short time before the Confederacy collapsed.

So why is this the flag everyone recognizes?

The familiar rectangular red flag with a blue X is derived from Confederate battle-flag designs. The Confederate Army never had one single official battle flag, and the famous Army of Northern Virginia version was square. Rectangular versions became especially common after the war because they were easier to manufacture and display at Confederate veterans’ reunions and in popular memory.

During the Civil War, battle-flag designs represented armies serving a Confederate government created by seceding slaveholding states in a crisis centered on slavery. After the war, the design became part of Confederate veteran remembrance and Lost Cause memory. During the 20th century it was also used by white segregationists resisting the civil-rights movement and federal desegregation. Some supporters today describe it as a symbol of Southern, family, or military heritage. Those different uses are part of the reason the flag remains controversial: a symbol can gather new meanings over time without losing the history it already carries.
