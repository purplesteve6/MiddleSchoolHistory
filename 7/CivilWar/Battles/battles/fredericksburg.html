Fredericksburg showed the deadly power of a strong defensive position: Union assaults on Marye’s Heights were a disaster.

Before the Fighting

A plan slowed by delay

Union General Ambrose Burnside hoped to move quickly across the Rappahannock River, seize Fredericksburg, and continue toward Richmond. His plan depended on speed. Instead, delays in getting pontoon bridges to the front gave Lee time to concentrate the Confederate army on strong defensive ground west of town.

Why the battlefield favored the defenders

Once the Union army crossed the river, it still faced a terrible military problem. Confederate troops occupied ridges and a stone wall at the base of Marye’s Heights. That meant Union infantry would have to attack across open ground into artillery and rifle fire. Fredericksburg helps students see why “bravery” is not enough when the ground and the weapons overwhelmingly favor the defense.

Ambrose Burnside

Burnside is remembered for more than this battle, but Fredericksburg became the defining military disaster of his command. His very recognizable facial hair also gave English the word “sideburns.”

How It Unfolded

1

Bridges built under fire

Union engineers came under heavy sniper fire while trying to lay pontoon bridges across the river. Eventually Union troops crossed into Fredericksburg and drove the Confederate sharpshooters from town.

2

Marye’s Heights turns deadly

On December 13, Burnside ordered wave after wave of Union soldiers forward against the stone wall at Marye’s Heights. The attacks were brave, but the position was too strong and the open approach too deadly.

3

The Union cannot break through

Again and again the assaults failed. By the end of the day, the ground in front of the Confederate line was littered with Union casualties. Burnside’s army had achieved almost nothing for the enormous cost it paid.

Lee is associated with the line, “It is well that war is so terrible, otherwise we should grow too fond of it.” Students often remember Fredericksburg because the battlefield makes that quote feel especially real.

Politics & the War

Why this mattered beyond the battlefield

The defeat badly damaged Burnside’s reputation. In early 1863 he was replaced by Joseph Hooker, reminding students that Civil War generals operated under intense political pressure. Failures in the field could end a commander’s career quickly.

Fredericksburg is a classic lesson in the power of defense. Strong ground plus prepared defenders can turn an attack into a disaster.

Why It Mattered

Fredericksburg strengthened Confederate confidence and deepened Northern frustration. More importantly for students, it provides one of the clearest battlefield examples of why frontal attacks against fortified positions could be so costly in the Civil War.
