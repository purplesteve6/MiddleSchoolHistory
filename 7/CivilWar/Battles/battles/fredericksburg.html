December 11–15, 1862 • Fredericksburg, Virginia

Fredericksburg

Repeated frontal attacks meet a nearly perfect defensive position.

Confederate victory
1862THE STONE WALLFredericksburg, Virginia

Battle at a Glance

DateDecember 11–15, 1862
LocationFredericksburg, Virginia
CommandersAmbrose Burnside • Robert E. Lee
ResultConfederate victory

Before the Fighting

Union General Ambrose Burnside hoped to move quickly toward Richmond by crossing the Rappahannock River at Fredericksburg. Delays in receiving pontoon bridges gave Lee time to concentrate his army on strong ground west of the city. The Union army crossed the river, but now faced Confederate defenders on ridges and behind a stone wall at Marye’s Heights.

How It Unfolded

1

Crossing Under Fire

Union engineers built pontoon bridges while Confederate sharpshooters fired from buildings in Fredericksburg. Union troops eventually crossed and occupied the city.

2

Assaults on Marye’s Heights

On December 13, wave after wave of Union infantry advanced across open ground toward the stone wall. Confederate rifles and artillery tore into the attackers.

3

Burnside Pulls Back

No Union assault broke the line. Burnside finally withdrew his army across the Rappahannock, leaving Lee with a clear victory.

The stone wall is still holding. Attack again?

Your troops must cross hundreds of yards of open ground while Confederate infantry fires from behind a stone wall and artillery fires from the heights.

Your choice: Preserve the remaining troops and look for another way around the position.
What happened: Burnside ordered repeated attacks. None reached the Confederate line. By the end of the day, thousands of Union soldiers had been killed or wounded in front of Marye’s Heights.

Why It Mattered

Fredericksburg became a lesson in the power of prepared defensive positions against frontal assault. The defeat badly damaged Union morale and Burnside’s reputation. It also showed a problem that armies would face again and again: improved rifles and artillery made crossing open ground against entrenched defenders increasingly deadly.

At Fredericksburg, the terrain and the defensive position mattered as much as courage.
