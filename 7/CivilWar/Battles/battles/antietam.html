September 17, 1862 • Sharpsburg, Maryland

Antietam

Lee’s invasion is stopped—and Lincoln changes the purpose of the war.

Strategic Union victory
1862ONE TERRIBLE DAYSharpsburg, Maryland

Battle at a Glance

DateSeptember 17, 1862
LocationSharpsburg, Maryland
CommandersGeorge B. McClellan • Robert E. Lee
ResultStrategic Union victory

Before the Fighting

After victories in Virginia, Robert E. Lee moved the Army of Northern Virginia into Maryland. He hoped a successful invasion might weaken Northern support for the war and perhaps encourage foreign recognition of the Confederacy. Union soldiers found a lost copy of Lee’s orders, giving George McClellan an unusual chance to understand how the Confederate army was divided.

How It Unfolded

1

Morning: Cornfield & Dunker Church

Union and Confederate attacks swept back and forth across a cornfield and around the small Dunker Church. Units were shattered in repeated assaults.

2

Midday: The Sunken Road

Fighting shifted to a farm lane that became known as the Bloody Lane. Union troops eventually broke the Confederate position, but did not destroy Lee’s army.

3

Afternoon: Burnside Bridge

Union troops crossed Antietam Creek and threatened Lee’s right. Confederate reinforcements from Harpers Ferry arrived in time to stop the advance.

Five Days Later

SEPT. 17

Antietam: Lee’s invasion is stopped and the Confederate army withdraws from Maryland.

→
SEPT. 22

Preliminary Emancipation Proclamation: Lincoln announces that enslaved people in areas still in rebellion on January 1 will be declared free.

Lincoln had already decided to move toward emancipation, but he waited for a military success so the announcement would not look like an act of desperation.

Why It Mattered

Antietam was the bloodiest single day in American military history. Lee withdrew to Virginia, allowing Lincoln to claim the result as a Union success. Five days later, Lincoln announced the preliminary Emancipation Proclamation. From that point forward, destroying slavery in the rebellious states became an official Union war aim as well as preserving the Union.

Antietam mattered because Lee’s invasion was stopped—and because Lincoln used the moment to make emancipation part of the war.
