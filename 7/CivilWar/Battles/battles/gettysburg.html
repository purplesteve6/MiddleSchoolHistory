ResultUnion victory

Before the Fighting

In June 1863, Lee led the Army of Northern Virginia north through Maryland and into Pennsylvania. He hoped to gather supplies, threaten Northern cities, weaken support for the war, and perhaps win a dramatic victory on Union soil. Neither army planned to fight at Gettysburg, but their roads brought them together there.

How It Unfolded

1

Day 1: Union Retreats Through Town

Confederate forces drove outnumbered Union troops back through Gettysburg. The Union army regrouped on strong high ground south of town.

2

Day 2: Attacks on Both Flanks

Lee attacked the Union left and right at places such as Little Round Top, the Wheatfield, the Peach Orchard, Culp’s Hill, and East Cemetery Hill. The Union line bent but did not break.

3

Day 3: Pickett’s Charge

After a huge artillery bombardment, about 12,500 Confederate soldiers advanced across open ground toward the Union center on Cemetery Ridge. The assault failed with devastating losses.

Day 3: How would you try to break the Union line?

The Union army holds high ground. Two days of attacks on the flanks have failed. Lee believes the Union center may now be weaker.

Lee’s choice: Attack the center after an artillery bombardment. The assault known as Pickett’s Charge crossed roughly three-quarters of a mile of open ground and was repulsed.
Alternative: Maneuvering might threaten Union supply lines, but it would require time and coordination while the Union army was also moving.
Alternative: Lee could preserve more of his army, but he would give up the chance for a dramatic victory on Northern soil.

Why It Mattered

Gettysburg ended Lee’s most ambitious invasion of the North. His army remained dangerous, but it would never again mount a comparable offensive into Union territory. Combined with the fall of Vicksburg on July 4, the battle marked a major shift in momentum toward the Union.

Gettysburg stopped Lee in the North; Vicksburg split the Confederacy in the West. Together, July 1863 changed the direction of the war.
