Before the Fighting

In June 1863, Lee led the Army of Northern Virginia into Pennsylvania. He hoped to gather supplies, threaten Northern cities, weaken support for the war, and perhaps win a dramatic victory on Union soil. The Union army, now led by George G. Meade, moved to intercept him.

Gettysburg is famous partly because it feels like a classic turning point. It was huge, bloody, and memorable. But it also mattered because of what it did not allow Lee to do: win recognition or a decisive triumph in the North.

Person to Know: George G. Meade

Meade had almost no time to settle into command before facing Lee. Winning at Gettysburg gave the Union the major eastern victory it desperately needed.

How It Unfolded

1

Day 1: Union Retreats Through Town

Confederate forces pushed back outnumbered Union troops, but the Union army regrouped on strong high ground south of Gettysburg.

2

Day 2: Attacks on Both Flanks

Lee struck at the Union left and right at places such as Little Round Top, the Wheatfield, the Peach Orchard, and Culp’s Hill. The Union line bent but did not break.

3

Day 3: Pickett’s Charge

After a huge artillery bombardment, about 12,500 Confederate soldiers advanced across open ground toward the Union center on Cemetery Ridge. The assault failed with devastating losses.

A popular story says Confederates came into Gettysburg partly looking for shoes. Whether or not shoes started the clash, a town where many roads met was exactly the kind of place where armies could collide by accident.

Politics & the War

Why this battle mattered beyond the battlefield

Meade had been placed in command of the Union army only days before the battle. Later in 1863, Lincoln’s Gettysburg Address would turn the battlefield into a place of national memory as well as military history.

Lee on Day 3: what would you do?

The Union army holds strong ground. Two days of fighting have not broken it.

Lee’s choice: Attack the center after an artillery bombardment. Pickett’s Charge failed with devastating losses.
Alternative: Maneuvering might threaten Union communications, but it would take time and the Union army was already concentrating.
Alternative: Lee could preserve strength, but he would give up the chance for a dramatic victory in the North.

Why It Mattered

Gettysburg ended Lee’s most ambitious invasion of the North. His army remained dangerous, but it would never again launch a comparable offensive into Union territory. Combined with the fall of Vicksburg on July 4, the battle marked a major shift in momentum toward the Union.

G and V together: Gettysburg in the East, Vicksburg in the West. Pair them to remember the double Union turning point of July 1863.
