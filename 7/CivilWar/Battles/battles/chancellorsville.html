Before the Fighting

Union General Joseph Hooker planned a bold campaign to outmaneuver Lee. His large army crossed rivers west of Fredericksburg and concentrated near Chancellorsville. Lee, badly outnumbered, responded with one of the riskiest decisions of the war.

Chancellorsville is often called Lee’s greatest victory, but it also shows students that a brilliant short-term win can still carry a heavy long-term cost.

Person to Know: Joseph Hooker

Hooker entered the campaign confident and well supplied, but he lost the initiative at the crucial moment. Chancellorsville is a reminder that numbers alone do not guarantee victory.

How It Unfolded

1

Hooker Loses Momentum

Hooker’s army gained an advantageous position, but he pulled back and allowed Lee time to react.

2

Jackson’s Flank March

Lee split his smaller army and sent Jackson on a long march around the Union right. The surprise attack smashed into the exposed Union flank late on May 2.

3

Victory with a Loss

The Confederates forced Hooker to retreat, but Stonewall Jackson was accidentally shot by his own men that night and later died.

Jackson was not hit by Union fire. He was accidentally shot by Confederate soldiers in the confusion after dark.

Politics & the War

Why this battle mattered beyond the battlefield

Hooker’s defeat ended his time in command. Just weeks later Lee headed north into Pennsylvania, and the Union government would again have to look for the right commander to stop him.

A commander can win a brilliant battle and still suffer a loss that matters later. Lee won the field, but Jackson was gone.

Why It Mattered

Lee had defeated a much larger army, but Jackson’s loss hurt the Confederacy badly. Chancellorsville also encouraged Lee to launch another invasion of the North, setting the stage for Gettysburg.
