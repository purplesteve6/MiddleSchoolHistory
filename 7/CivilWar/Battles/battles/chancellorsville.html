Chancellorsville was one of Lee’s greatest victories, but the Confederacy lost Stonewall Jackson.

Before the Fighting

Hooker’s strong position

Union General Joseph Hooker had a large, well-equipped army and a promising plan. By crossing rivers and moving west of Fredericksburg, he hoped to outmaneuver Lee and force the Confederates into a bad position. On paper, Hooker seemed to have the advantage in numbers, supply, and initiative.

Why Lee took such a risk

Lee responded with one of the boldest decisions of the war. Even though he was badly outnumbered, he divided his army in the face of the enemy. That was dangerous, but Lee believed Hooker had become hesitant and that surprise could overcome numbers. Chancellorsville is important because it shows how leadership and nerve sometimes shaped battles as much as raw manpower.

Robert E. Lee

Chancellorsville is often called Lee’s greatest victory. He took enormous risks, split his smaller army, and still defeated a much larger Union force.

How It Unfolded

1

Hooker gives up the initiative

After moving into a promising position, Hooker stopped advancing and pulled back into the tangled Wilderness area. That decision gave Lee time to think and act.

2

Jackson marches around the Union flank

Lee sent Stonewall Jackson on a daring march around the Union right. Late on May 2, Jackson’s men crashed into an exposed Union flank, creating panic and confusion.

3

Victory shadowed by loss

The Confederates forced Hooker to retreat, but the triumph came with a terrible price. Jackson was accidentally shot by Confederate soldiers in the dark and later died of complications.

Jackson was not struck by Union fire. He was accidentally shot by his own men after nightfall, one of the most famous friendly-fire incidents in American history.

Politics & the War

Why this mattered beyond the battlefield

Hooker’s defeat ended his time in command. Chancellorsville also encouraged Lee to take the offensive again and invade Pennsylvania. That decision would lead directly to Gettysburg.

A commander can win a famous battle and still suffer a loss that matters later. Lee held the field, but Jackson was gone.

Why It Mattered

Chancellorsville preserved Lee’s reputation as a daring battlefield commander, yet it also weakened the Confederacy by costing it Stonewall Jackson. It was a brilliant short-term victory paired with a serious long-term loss.
