Before the Fighting

By spring 1865, Grant’s armies had pinned Lee around Petersburg and Richmond. When Union forces finally broke the Confederate lines, Lee abandoned both cities and tried to move west toward supplies and another Confederate army. Union cavalry and infantry raced ahead and blocked his route near Appomattox Court House.

How It Unfolded

1

The Escape Route Closes

On the morning of April 9, Confederate troops attacked, hoping to break through. They discovered that large Union forces stood in their path.

2

Lee Asks to Meet Grant

Lee concluded that continued fighting would only cause more deaths. He sent word to Grant asking to discuss surrender.

3

The McLean House

Lee and Grant met in Wilmer McLean’s home. Grant allowed Confederate soldiers to return home after surrendering their weapons; officers could keep sidearms, and men who owned horses or mules could take them home for spring planting.

Grant’s terms allowed surrendered soldiers to return home and promised they would not be disturbed by U.S. authorities as long as they obeyed the laws where they lived.

Paraphrase of the surrender terms Grant wrote at Appomattox, April 9, 1865.

Not the Last Surrender

Appomattox was the most famous surrender because Lee commanded the Confederacy’s best-known army. But the Civil War did not literally end everywhere on April 9. Joseph E. Johnston surrendered a large Confederate force to Sherman in North Carolina later that month, and other commands followed.

Why It Mattered

Lee surrendered roughly 28,000 soldiers of the Army of Northern Virginia. Other Confederate forces were still in the field, but their major surrenders followed in the weeks ahead. Appomattox became the symbol of the military end of the Civil War—and the beginning of the much harder question of how the country would reunite and define freedom after slavery.

Appomattox ended Lee’s war, not every question the war had created.
