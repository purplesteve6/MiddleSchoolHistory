Before the Fighting

After South Carolina seceded, U.S. Major Robert Anderson moved his small garrison into Fort Sumter, a federal fort in Charleston Harbor. The new Confederate government insisted that the fort sat inside Confederate territory and demanded that Anderson leave. President Abraham Lincoln faced a choice that would shape the nation: abandon federal property or try to supply a garrison that was running out of food.

Fort Sumter makes sense only if students remember that the Civil War began as a crisis over secession. Eleven slave states eventually left the Union and created the Confederate States of America after Lincoln’s election. Confederate leaders claimed they were defending their independence; the U.S. government said secession was illegal and that the Union had to be preserved.

Person to Know: Abraham Lincoln

Lincoln tried to resupply Fort Sumter without firing the first shot. Once the Confederates opened fire, he could present the Union as defending federal property rather than starting the war.

How It Unfolded

1

A Fort Running Out of Supplies

By April 1861, Anderson’s men were short on food. Lincoln announced that he would send provisions to the fort, not an invading army.

2

Confederate Guns Open Fire

At 4:30 a.m. on April 12, Confederate batteries opened fire. Fort Sumter answered, but the small federal garrison could not match the ring of guns around the harbor.

3

The Garrison Evacuates

After about 34 hours of bombardment, Anderson agreed to evacuate. The Confederacy had won the first military confrontation of the war.

The first deaths connected to Fort Sumter happened after the fighting, when a cannon exploded during a 100-gun salute as the Union garrison prepared to leave.

Politics & the War

Why this battle mattered beyond the battlefield

After Fort Sumter, compromise collapsed fast. Lincoln’s call for volunteers convinced several Upper South states to secede. That matters because the Civil War would now include the large, strategically important states of Virginia and Tennessee.

A battle can be historically important even when it is small if it changes what happens next.

Why It Mattered

The bombardment did not kill soldiers in combat, but its political effect was enormous. Lincoln called for 75,000 volunteers to put down the rebellion. Virginia, Arkansas, Tennessee, and North Carolina then joined the Confederacy. A national crisis that had been argued over in speeches and conventions was now a war.

Sumter = Start. The “S” in Sumter helps remember that this was where the shooting started.
