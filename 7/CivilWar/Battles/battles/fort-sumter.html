Before the Fighting

After South Carolina seceded, U.S. Major Robert Anderson moved his small garrison into Fort Sumter, a federal fort in Charleston Harbor. The new Confederate government claimed the fort stood inside its territory and demanded that Anderson leave. President Abraham Lincoln faced a dangerous choice: abandon federal property or try to supply a garrison that was running out of food.

How It Unfolded

1

A Fort Running Out of Supplies

By April 1861, Anderson’s men were short on food. Lincoln announced that he would send provisions to the fort, not an army to attack Charleston.

2

Confederate Guns Open Fire

At 4:30 a.m. on April 12, Confederate batteries began bombarding Fort Sumter. The fort returned fire, but its small garrison could not match the guns surrounding the harbor.

3

The Garrison Surrenders

After about 34 hours of bombardment, Anderson agreed to evacuate. The Confederacy had won the first military confrontation of the war.

What would you do with Fort Sumter?

It is March 1861. The fort belongs to the United States, but it is surrounded by a state that says it has left the Union. The garrison is running out of food.

Possible advantage: You avoid an immediate clash—but abandoning the fort might look like accepting secession.
Lincoln’s choice: Send supplies and notify South Carolina that the mission was to provision the fort. Confederate leaders decided to fire before the expedition could succeed.
Possible advantage: A strong force might defend federal authority—but it could also make the United States appear to be starting the shooting.

Why It Mattered

No soldiers were killed by enemy fire during the bombardment, but the effect was enormous. Lincoln called for 75,000 volunteers to put down the rebellion. Four more slave states—Virginia, Arkansas, Tennessee, and North Carolina—then joined the Confederacy. A crisis that had been political was now a war.

Fort Sumter did not decide the war—it made the war impossible to avoid.
