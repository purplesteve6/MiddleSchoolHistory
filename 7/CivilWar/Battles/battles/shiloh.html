The Battle of Shiloh, fought near Pittsburg Landing in April 1862.
Shiloh / Pittsburg Landing, Tennessee, along the Tennessee River.

Shiloh showed how bloody the war would become and helped establish Ulysses S. Grant as a general who would keep fighting.

Before the Fighting

A western campaign opens up

Early Union victories at Forts Henry and Donelson had weakened Confederate control in Tennessee. Grant gathered his army near Pittsburg Landing on the Tennessee River while waiting for Buell’s troops to join him. Confederate General Albert Sidney Johnston decided he had to strike before the two Union armies could fully combine.

Why Shiloh was a warning

Some people still hoped the war could be won through a few bold actions and quick campaigns. Shiloh showed how unrealistic that hope was becoming. Two large armies were about to collide in the West, far from the most famous fighting in Virginia, reminding students that the Civil War was a national conflict fought in multiple theaters.

Ulysses S. Grant

Grant’s army was surprised on the first morning, but he refused to panic or give up. His ability to recover from a terrible first day and keep fighting helped build the reputation that eventually carried him to command of all Union armies.

How It Unfolded

1

A surprise attack at dawn

On April 6, Confederate troops crashed into Union camps and drove many soldiers back in confusion. The first day of Shiloh did not look like a Union success at all; it looked like a near disaster.

2

The Union line bends but does not break

Fierce fighting raged in places later remembered as the Hornet’s Nest and the Peach Orchard. Grant’s army lost ground but avoided total collapse. Confederate commander Albert Sidney Johnston was shot in the leg and bled to death after failing to realize how serious the wound was; P. G. T. Beauregard then took command.

3

Reinforcements reverse the battle

Don Carlos Buell’s Union reinforcements arrived during the night. On April 7, Beauregard discovered that the Union army was now much stronger and increasingly outnumbered his exhausted force. The Union counterattacked, and the Confederates finally retreated toward Corinth.

The battlefield took its name from a small country church called Shiloh. One part of the field became famous as the “Hornet’s Nest” because of the intense buzzing sound of bullets.

Politics & the War

Why this mattered beyond the battlefield

Some Northern newspapers criticized Grant after the first day’s surprise, but Lincoln did not throw him aside. Grant’s ability to recover and keep fighting mattered politically because the Union still needed aggressive commanders who would not collapse after setbacks.

Shiloh shows that a battle can be a “victory” and still feel horrifying because of the number of casualties it produces.

Why It Mattered

Shiloh revealed how bloody the western war could become. It also strengthened Union control in Tennessee and showed that battles outside the East could reshape the war just as much as the famous clashes around Washington and Richmond.
