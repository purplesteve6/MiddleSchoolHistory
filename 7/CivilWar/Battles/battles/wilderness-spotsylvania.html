Before the Fighting

Grant comes east

In 1864, Lincoln brought Ulysses S. Grant east and put him in charge of all Union armies. Grant worked with George G. Meade against Lee’s Army of Northern Virginia. He did not expect a quick, clean triumph. Instead, he planned to keep pressure on Lee continuously, using the Union’s larger manpower and resources over time.

Why these battles belong together

The Wilderness and Spotsylvania are linked because the most important story is what happened between them: Grant did not retreat north after the first brutal battle. He moved south instead. That decision told soldiers, politicians, and the public that the Union was now fighting a relentless campaign rather than hoping for one dramatic breakthrough.

Spotsylvania Court House: May 8-19, 1864

Spotsylvania followed immediately after the Wilderness. Confederate troops dug strong defensive earthworks, and Grant repeatedly tried to break or outflank Lee’s line as the Overland Campaign continued south toward Richmond.

Term to Know

Attrition

Attrition means wearing down an enemy through steady losses in soldiers and resources. Grant believed the Union could endure this kind of campaign better than the Confederacy could.

How It Unfolded

1

The Wilderness burns and confuses both armies

Fighting began in dense woods where visibility was terrible. Smoke, brush, and accidental fires made control difficult and intensified the horror. The battle ended without a clear tactical winner.

2

Grant marches south instead of back

After earlier bloody fights, Union armies often withdrew and reset. After the Wilderness, Grant moved south toward Spotsylvania. That one decision is central to understanding why the campaign mattered.

3

Spotsylvania deepens the grind

At Spotsylvania, both sides dug in. On May 12, a massive Union attack broke into a curved part of the Confederate defenses. For roughly 20 hours, soldiers fought at extremely close range in rain and mud at the place later called the “Bloody Angle.” The battle remained inconclusive, but the relentless losses fit Grant’s larger strategy of attrition.

Brush fires ignited during the Wilderness battle trapped some wounded soldiers between the lines, making it one of the most terrifying battlefields of the war.

Politics & the War

Why this mattered beyond the battlefield

Lincoln faced reelection in 1864, and Northern morale mattered politically. Grant’s strategy signaled that the Union was finally matching military operations to its larger resources. Even when losses were high, the campaign showed persistence rather than drift.

Wilderness and Spotsylvania are the clearest middle-school example of attrition: not a single dramatic breakthrough, but a campaign designed to keep wearing the enemy down.

Why It Mattered

These battles did not produce a neat battlefield triumph, but they changed the direction of the war. Grant kept moving, Lee kept losing irreplaceable men, and the Union increasingly fought on terms that favored its greater strength.
