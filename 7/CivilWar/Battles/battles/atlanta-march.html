Before the Fighting

Atlanta as a military and political target

Sherman spent the summer of 1864 fighting and maneuvering toward Atlanta, a major railroad center and industrial city. The campaign mattered because Atlanta was not just another place name on a map. It was a transportation hub and a symbol. Capturing it would damage Confederate logistics and give the North badly needed good news.

Why the story continues after the city falls

Sherman did not stop with Atlanta. After taking the city, he launched the March to the Sea, cutting loose from his traditional supply line and moving across Georgia toward Savannah. That is why this page treats Atlanta and the march together. The city’s fall matters, but the larger lesson is how late-war strategy targeted the Confederacy’s ability and will to resist.

How Sherman planned to feed the army

Sherman cut loose from a traditional supply line and ordered his army to “live off the land.” Foraging parties took food, livestock, and supplies from farms along the route. Soldiers sent out to gather these supplies were often called “bummers.”

Hard war and scorched earth

Hard war meant attacking not only enemy armies, but also the railroads, warehouses, mills, crops, and supplies that allowed those armies to keep fighting. Sherman’s march also included scorched-earth tactics: deliberate destruction of resources the Union believed supported Confederate resistance. This was broader than a normal battlefield victory, but it was not the same as unlimited violence against every civilian.

William T. Sherman

Sherman believed that defeating Confederate armies was not enough by 1864. The Union also had to destroy the transportation, supplies, and confidence that allowed the Confederacy to continue the war.

How It Unfolded

1

Sherman closes in on Atlanta

Through the summer, Sherman used movement and pressure to force Confederate retreats and fight a series of engagements. Confederate command shifted from Joseph E. Johnston to the more aggressive John Bell Hood, but Hood’s attacks could not stop the Union advance.

2

Atlanta falls

On September 2, 1864, Union forces gained control of Atlanta after the Confederates evacuated the city. Capturing this major railroad and industrial center badly hurt Confederate morale and gave Northern voters dramatic evidence that the Union was making progress.

3

The March to the Sea begins

Sherman then marched from Atlanta across Georgia to Savannah, destroying railroads, warehouses, mills, crops, and other resources that supported the Confederate war effort. Savannah fell in December. Sherman informed Lincoln that he was presenting the captured city as a Christmas gift to the president.

Sherman’s men twisted heated railroad rails around trees, creating bent metal nicknamed “Sherman’s neckties.”

Politics & the War

Why this mattered beyond the battlefield

The fall of Atlanta helped Lincoln win reelection in 1864. Without that victory, Northern war weariness might have pushed voters toward a candidate more open to negotiated peace. In other words, what happened in Georgia affected what happened at the ballot box.

Sherman’s problem: how do you end resistance?

If an enemy’s civilians grow food, repair railroads, and sustain the government behind an army, what becomes a military target?

Too narrow for Sherman’s strategy: this leaves much of the Confederacy’s support system untouched.
Sherman’s logic: destroy the resources that keep armies moving and fighting. That is why historians often call this hard war.
Too broad: Sherman’s campaign was destructive, but it was not an unlimited license to attack everything and everyone without distinction.

Why It Mattered

Atlanta and the March to the Sea show how the Civil War changed by 1864. The Union was no longer focused only on defeating Confederate armies in set battles. It was also trying to break the systems that allowed those armies and the Confederate state to keep fighting.
