Atlanta & Sherman’s March

Union armies attack Confederate troops, railroads, industry, supplies—and morale.

Before the Fighting

While Grant fought Lee in Virginia, William T. Sherman led a second major Union offensive from Chattanooga into Georgia. Atlanta was one of the Confederacy’s most important railroad and industrial centers. Capturing it would damage the Southern war effort and give the North a badly needed victory during the 1864 presidential election year.

How It Unfolded

1

The Atlanta Campaign

Sherman repeatedly maneuvered around Confederate positions, forcing Joseph E. Johnston back toward Atlanta. Confederate President Jefferson Davis replaced Johnston with the more aggressive John Bell Hood.

2

Atlanta Falls

Hood attacked several times but failed to stop Sherman. After Union forces cut key railroads, the Confederate army evacuated Atlanta on September 1. Union troops entered the city the next day.

3

The March to the Sea

After Lincoln’s reelection, Sherman left Atlanta in November with about 60,000 soldiers and marched toward Savannah. His army lived partly off the land and destroyed railroads, warehouses, mills, crops, and other resources that supported the Confederate war effort.

“Total War” — or Hard War?

Many school textbooks describe Sherman’s strategy as total war. Historians often use the more precise term hard war.

What Sherman targeted

Railroads, bridges, warehouses, mills, crops, livestock, factories, and other resources that could feed, move, or equip Confederate armies.

What made it different

The campaign deliberately brought the cost of war into the Southern home front. It aimed to damage not only armies but also the Confederacy’s ability—and will—to keep fighting.

Why the wording matters

In later 20th-century “total wars,” entire civilian populations could be treated as direct military targets. Sherman’s destruction was severe and civilians suffered greatly, but killing civilians was not the stated purpose of his campaign.

When railroads, farms, and factories keep an army fighting, do they become legitimate military targets?

Why It Mattered

Atlanta’s fall helped convince Northern voters that Union victory was possible and strengthened Lincoln before the 1864 election. The March to the Sea then carried “hard war” deep into Georgia, damaging transportation, supplies, and civilian confidence in the Confederate government’s ability to protect them. Savannah fell in December.

Sherman’s goal was not only to defeat armies. He wanted to make it harder for the Confederacy to keep armies supplied and to keep the war going.
