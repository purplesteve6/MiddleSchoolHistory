Before the Fighting

Sherman spent the summer of 1864 fighting and maneuvering his way toward Atlanta, a crucial railroad and manufacturing center. After the city fell, he launched the famous March to the Sea, leaving his supply line and moving across Georgia toward Savannah.

This page helps explain how the Civil War changed by 1864. The goal was no longer just to defeat an opposing army in one field battle. Sherman aimed to damage the Confederacy’s ability and willingness to keep fighting.

Person to Know: William T. Sherman

Sherman believed that ending the war faster required breaking the Confederacy’s ability to continue it. His campaign is essential for understanding how strategy and politics came together late in the war.

How It Unfolded

1

The Fight for Atlanta

Sherman advanced through north Georgia while Confederate commanders Joseph E. Johnston and then John Bell Hood tried to stop him. Atlanta fell in early September.

2

Why Atlanta Mattered

Atlanta was a rail hub and symbolically important city. Its loss hurt Confederate morale and boosted Northern confidence.

3

The March to the Sea

Sherman marched from Atlanta to Savannah, destroying railroads, warehouses, crops, and other resources that supported the Confederate war effort.

Sherman’s men twisted heated railroad rails around trees, creating bent metal known as “Sherman’s neckties.”

Politics & the War

Why this battle mattered beyond the battlefield

Without the capture of Atlanta, Lincoln might have lost the 1864 election to a candidate more willing to negotiate peace. That would have changed the political future of both the Union and emancipation.

Sherman’s problem: how do you end resistance?

If an enemy’s civilians grow food, repair railroads, and support the government keeping armies in the field, what becomes a military target?

Too narrow for Sherman’s strategy: this would leave the Confederacy’s support system largely intact.
Sherman’s logic: destroy the resources that keep armies moving and fighting. This is why historians often talk about “hard war.”
Too broad: Sherman’s campaign was destructive, but it was not a license for unlimited violence against everything and everyone.

Why It Mattered

The fall of Atlanta helped Lincoln win reelection in 1864. Sherman’s march also showed the logic of what textbooks often call “total war,” though many historians prefer the more precise term “hard war”: attacking the resources and infrastructure that sustained the enemy’s armies.

Atlanta and the March = break the railroads, break the resistance.
