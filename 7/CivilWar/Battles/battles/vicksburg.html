Before the Fighting

Vicksburg sat on high bluffs above the Mississippi River. As long as the Confederacy held it, the Union did not fully control the great river. Grant spent months maneuvering, marching, and fighting to isolate the city before settling into a siege.

Vicksburg helps students see why geography mattered so much. A city can become a military prize because of where it is, not simply because of its size.

Person to Know: Ulysses S. Grant

Vicksburg was one of Grant’s greatest achievements. The campaign showed not just toughness, but also flexibility and strategic imagination.

How It Unfolded

1

Grant Moves South of the City

After several failed efforts from the north, Grant marched his army down the west bank of the Mississippi, crossed below Vicksburg, and came at the city from the east.

2

Campaign Successes Isolate Vicksburg

Grant won several battles inland and cut Vicksburg off from help.

3

Siege and Surrender

After two failed assaults, Grant settled into a siege. On July 4, 1863, Pemberton surrendered the city and its garrison.

Many Vicksburg civilians took shelter in caves dug into the hillsides to escape the bombardment.

Politics & the War

Why this battle mattered beyond the battlefield

Vicksburg greatly strengthened Grant’s reputation. He had shown that he could combine movement, aggression, and persistence—qualities Lincoln increasingly valued in his generals.

Controlling a river can reshape an entire war. Vicksburg mattered because it changed geography into power.

Why It Mattered

The Union now controlled the Mississippi River, splitting the Confederacy in two and badly damaging Confederate transportation and supply. Combined with Gettysburg, Vicksburg made July 1863 a disastrous month for the Confederacy.

Vicksburg = Victory on the Mississippi. The “V” helps link Vicksburg to victory and the river.
