March 8–9, 1862 • Hampton Roads, Virginia

Hampton Roads

Two strange new warships change naval warfare forever.

Before the Fighting

The Union navy was blockading the Confederate coast. At Norfolk, the Confederacy rebuilt the burned U.S. steam frigate Merrimack as an armored warship named CSS Virginia. On March 8, Virginia steamed into Hampton Roads and devastated wooden Union warships. That night, a newly arrived Union ironclad—the USS Monitor—took position to stop it.

How It Unfolded

1

Virginia Attacks the Wooden Fleet

On March 8, CSS Virginia sank USS Cumberland, forced USS Congress to surrender, and threatened the rest of the Union fleet.

2

Monitor Arrives

USS Monitor looked unlike a traditional warship: it sat very low in the water and carried two large guns in a rotating armored turret.

3

Iron Meets Iron

On March 9, Monitor and Virginia hammered each other for hours at close range. Neither could seriously damage the other, and the duel ended without a clear winner.

Old Navy / New Navy

Wooden Warship

The old standard

  • Wooden hull
  • Many guns firing from the sides
  • Sails, steam, or both
  • Vulnerable to explosive shells and heavy cannon fire
Ironclad

The new direction

  • Iron armor protects vital parts of the ship
  • Steam power does not depend on wind
  • Monitor: rotating gun turret
  • Virginia: sloped armored casemate and iron ram
Merrimack or Virginia? The ship had been the U.S. frigate Merrimack. The Confederacy rebuilt it and commissioned it as CSS Virginia. Many Northern newspapers kept calling it “Merrimack,” which is why both names still appear.

Why It Mattered

The battle itself was a draw, but the technology was decisive. Armor protected both ships from gunfire that could destroy wooden vessels. Navies around the world now knew that the age of the wooden warship was ending and that steam-powered armored ships were the future.

Hampton Roads was indecisive on the scoreboard and revolutionary in its impact.
