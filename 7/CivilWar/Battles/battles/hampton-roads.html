Before the Fighting

The Union blockade and a Confederate answer

The Union navy was trying to choke Southern trade by blockading Confederate ports. At Norfolk, Confederates raised the burned hull of the former USS Merrimack and rebuilt it as an ironclad, officially renaming it CSS Virginia. On this site, we use the familiar name Merrimack (CSS Virginia). On March 8, Merrimack steamed into Hampton Roads and smashed wooden Union ships that could do little against its iron armor.

From USS Merrimack to Merrimack (CSS Virginia)

When Union forces abandoned the Norfolk Navy Yard, they burned and sank the steam frigate USS Merrimack. Confederate forces salvaged the hull and rebuilt the ship as an ironclad, officially commissioning it as CSS Virginia. Because generations of students have known the ship by its original name, this page refers to it as Merrimack (CSS Virginia).

Why this battle mattered before it even ended

Overnight, the Union’s own ironclad, the USS Monitor, arrived to confront Virginia. That meant the battle was no longer only about one harbor or one fleet. It had become a test of whether armor, steam power, and new ship design were making traditional wooden warships obsolete. Students do not need to remember every maneuver here; they should remember the technology shift.

What happened to the two ironclads?

Neither famous ship survived 1862. Confederate sailors destroyed Merrimack after Union forces threatened Norfolk and the ship could not escape upriver. USS Monitor later sank in a storm off Cape Hatteras, North Carolina, on December 31, 1862.

Technology to Know

Ironclads

Ironclads were steam-powered warships protected by iron armor. At Hampton Roads, they proved that old wooden fleets were becoming outdated.

How It Unfolded

1

Merrimack attacks the wooden fleet

On March 8, Merrimack rammed and sank USS Cumberland, destroyed USS Congress, and terrified the rest of the Union ships in the harbor. It looked as though the Confederates had found a weapon that could overturn naval balance.

2

Monitor arrives just in time

The next morning the Union ironclad USS Monitor moved into position. It rode unusually low in the water and carried only two large guns, but those guns sat inside a revolving armored turret that could aim without turning the whole ship. Its iron armor let it stand up to Virginia in a way wooden ships could not.

3

Iron meets iron

For hours the two warships pounded each other at close range. Neither could seriously destroy the other. The battle ended without a clear tactical winner, but everyone watching understood that naval warfare had changed.

People sometimes called the USS Monitor a “cheesebox on a raft” because its low deck and round turret looked so unusual.

Politics & the War

Why this mattered beyond the battlefield

Hampton Roads connects directly to the Union blockade, part of the broader strategy often called the Anaconda Plan. If the Union could control the sea and the Confederacy could not break that control, Southern trade and supply would suffer badly over time.

Hampton Roads teaches an important lesson: a battle can be a draw on the battlefield and still be a turning point in technology.

Why It Mattered

The battle itself was inconclusive, but its larger meaning was clear. Navies around the world learned that armored steam warships represented the future. For students, Hampton Roads is essential because it connects the Civil War to innovation, not just infantry fighting on land.
