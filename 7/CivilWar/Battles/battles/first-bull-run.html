Before the Fighting

Many people in the North expected one dramatic victory to crush the rebellion. Union General Irvin McDowell led an inexperienced army toward Manassas Junction, an important railroad center in Virginia. Confederate troops waited nearby, and reinforcements under Joseph E. Johnston arrived by railroad from the Shenandoah Valley.

This battle helps explain why the Civil War lasted four years. In 1861, both civilians and politicians still imagined that enthusiasm could make up for inexperience. Bull Run showed that raw recruits, weak planning, and confusion could turn a promising attack into a collapse.

Person to Know: Stonewall Jackson

Jackson’s stubborn stand made him famous. His nickname, “Stonewall,” became one of the best-known names of the Civil War.

How It Unfolded

1

Union Troops Gain Ground

McDowell’s soldiers crossed Bull Run and drove back part of the Confederate line. For a time, a Union victory seemed possible.

2

Jackson Holds on Henry Hill

Confederate troops rallied around Thomas J. Jackson. His stand helped earn him the nickname “Stonewall,” and fresh Confederate units stiffened the line.

3

Retreat Turns into Rout

Union troops broke and fled toward Washington. The Confederate army held the field, though it was too disorganized to destroy the retreating Union army.

Some civilians rode out from Washington with picnic baskets to watch the battle, expecting easy entertainment. Instead they got a panicked Union retreat racing back toward the capital.

Politics & the War

Why this battle mattered beyond the battlefield

The defeat helped bring George B. McClellan to prominence in the East. Northern leaders now understood that they needed a more organized army and longer-term planning.

The most important lesson of First Bull Run was not who won one day—it was that the war would require huge armies and long effort.

Why It Mattered

Bull Run destroyed the idea that the war would be short or easy. Both governments now had to build larger, better-trained armies. The battle also showed how important railroads would be in moving troops quickly.

Bull Run burst the bubble. It burst the bubble of the “quick war” idea.
