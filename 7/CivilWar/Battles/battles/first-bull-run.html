First Bull Run / Manassas

Both sides discover that victory will not be easy.

Before the Fighting

Many people in the North expected one major victory to crush the rebellion. Union General Irvin McDowell marched an inexperienced army toward the important railroad junction at Manassas, about 30 miles from Washington, D.C. Confederate forces waited nearby, and reinforcements under Joseph E. Johnston arrived by railroad from the Shenandoah Valley.

How It Unfolded

1

Union Troops Push Forward

McDowell’s troops crossed Bull Run and drove back part of the Confederate line. For a time, a Union victory looked possible.

2

The Line Holds on Henry Hill

Confederate troops rallied around Brigadier General Thomas J. Jackson. His stand helped earn him the nickname “Stonewall,” and fresh Confederate units entered the fight.

3

A Retreat Becomes a Rout

Union troops broke and fled toward Washington. The Confederate army was too exhausted and disorganized to launch a major pursuit, but it held the battlefield.

Battlefield Confusion

First Bull Run exposed an unexpected problem: the Confederate Stars and Bars could look too much like the U.S. Stars and Stripes through smoke, dust, and folds in the cloth. The need for a more recognizable battlefield flag helped produce the square battle flag later carried by the Army of Northern Virginia.

Why It Mattered

First Bull Run destroyed the idea that the Civil War would be short or easy. Both governments began building larger, better-trained armies. The battle also showed the military importance of railroads, which helped the Confederates move reinforcements to the battlefield in time.

Bull Run taught both sides the same lesson: this war would take more than one battle.
