Road to War

During the 1850s, Americans repeatedly tried to settle the slavery question. Instead, each new compromise, law, court decision, act of violence, and election made the division between sections harder to contain.

The argument was not simply about whether slavery already existed in the South. The explosive question was whether slavery would be protected, expanded, restricted, or eventually ended.

The Crisis, Step by Step

1850

Compromise of 1850

Congress tried to calm the slavery debate with a package of laws. California entered as a free state, while a stronger Fugitive Slave Act required federal cooperation in capturing people who escaped slavery. The compromise reduced tension for a time but satisfied neither side for long.

1854

Kansas-Nebraska Act

The law allowed settlers in Kansas and Nebraska to vote on slavery themselves. It effectively overturned the old Missouri Compromise restriction and sent proslavery and antislavery settlers racing into Kansas.

1855–1856

Bleeding Kansas

Competing governments, election fraud, raids, and killings turned the territory into a preview of civil war. John Brown became nationally known during the violence.

1857

Dred Scott Decision

The Supreme Court ruled that Black Americans could not be U.S. citizens under the Constitution and that Congress lacked power to prohibit slavery in federal territories. Antislavery Northerners feared the decision could allow slavery to expand almost anywhere.

1859

John Brown at Harpers Ferry

Brown tried to seize federal weapons and spark a larger fight against slavery. The raid failed quickly, but the reaction was enormous. Go directly to the Harpers Ferry page →

Nov. 1860

Lincoln Is Elected

Abraham Lincoln won the presidency without carrying a single slave state. His Republican Party opposed the expansion of slavery into the territories. Many white Southern leaders concluded that their political power—and slavery's future—was no longer safe inside the Union.

1860–1861

Secession

South Carolina seceded first, followed by other Deep South states. They formed the Confederate States of America. After fighting began, four more slave states joined them, bringing the total to eleven.

What about “states' rights”? Secessionists did argue that states had rights the federal government could not violate. But the right their leaders most urgently defended in 1860–61 was the protection of slavery. Their speeches, laws, and secession documents repeatedly placed slavery at the center of the crisis.

Then the Shooting Started

Even after secession, one huge question remained: would the United States accept the Confederacy as an independent country? Federal forts still stood inside states that had seceded. At Fort Sumter in Charleston Harbor, Confederate forces opened fire on April 12, 1861. Lincoln called for troops, and the Civil War began.
