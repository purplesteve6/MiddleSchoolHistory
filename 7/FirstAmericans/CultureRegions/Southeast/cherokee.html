Peoples of the Southeast

Cherokee

From southern Appalachian towns to a nation in print: Cherokee history brings language, family, sovereignty, and survival into one story.

The Cherokee used language, writing, diplomacy, and law to strengthen their nation and defend their homeland. Forced removal caused devastating losses, but Cherokee nations continue today.

Mountain homelands, river-valley towns

Cherokee homelands spread across the southern Appalachians and surrounding areas, including parts of present-day North Carolina, Tennessee, Georgia, and neighboring states. Mountains did not keep people isolated: rivers, paths, and relationships connected towns.

Valley land offered places for fields and communities. Corn, beans, and squash joined hunted animals, fish, nuts, and gathered plants in a varied food system. Forest knowledge mattered as much as knowledge of farming.

Towns were centers of decision-making and community life. Cherokee history begins long before American settlers arrived or a newspaper was printed. Those later developments grew from an existing people with their own language and institutions.

Family through the mother’s line

Traditional Cherokee society includes seven clans. A person’s clan comes through the mother’s line: this is matrilineal organization.

A clan could connect people living in different towns. Family identity included responsibilities to other people, not just a last name. Clan relationships shaped marriage and community life.

These traditions have changed over time, and knowledge varies among families. Tribal citizenship and clan identity are different things. A modern Cherokee person does not have to fit one picture of the past to belong to a living nation.

Knowledge woven into everyday life

River-cane baskets combine local plant knowledge with skilled design. Makers select and prepare material, split it into strips, and weave forms suitable for carrying and storing. Patterns and techniques reflect teaching passed among people over generations.

Homes, tools, food preparation, and art all required expertise. It is easy to notice a finished basket and miss the many decisions behind it: when to gather cane, how to make even strips, how to work with dyes, and how to build a strong shape.

Basket-making continues today. Contemporary artists can preserve inherited techniques while creating new work. Keeping a tradition alive does not require every object to look exactly like an old example.

Sequoyah

Sequoyah developed a way to write Cherokee, completing his system around 1821. Instead of making one symbol for each separate sound as in the English alphabet, his system uses characters that represent syllables.

It is called a syllabary. Once people learned the characters, they could record words in their own language. Cherokee speakers used the system for letters, reading, printing, and other purposes.

The achievement was more than adopting someone else’s writing. Sequoyah created a tool that made Cherokee speech visible on a page. The language already existed; he developed a writing system for it.

The Cherokee Phoenix: a nation in print

In 1828, the Cherokee Phoenix began publication at New Echota, the Cherokee national capital in present-day Georgia. The newspaper printed material in Cherokee and English.

It reported news and helped communicate Cherokee views to readers inside and outside the nation. A printing press could carry arguments farther than a speaker could travel. Writing became one tool in the struggle over land and political rights.

The Cherokee Phoenix was the first Native American newspaper in the United States. It made Cherokee language and political arguments visible in a format that could reach many communities.

Changing institutions, defending a homeland

In the early 1800s, the Cherokee Nation strengthened national institutions and adopted a written constitution in 1827. Many leaders hoped that a national government, schools, publishing, and diplomacy would help protect their people’s future.

White settlers and state officials still wanted Cherokee land. Pressure increased as cotton farming expanded and gold drew interest to northern Georgia. Adopting institutions familiar to Americans did not protect Cherokee families from demands that they leave.

The central issue was sovereignty: could Georgia impose its authority inside Cherokee territory? In Worcester v. Georgia in 1832, the U.S. Supreme Court ruled against Georgia’s attempt to apply its laws there. A legal victory did not make the threat of removal disappear.

A disputed treaty, then forced removal

In 1835, a small Cherokee faction signed the Treaty of New Echota, agreeing to give up eastern lands. Principal Chief John Ross and most Cherokee people opposed it. The United States used the treaty to proceed with removal despite that opposition.

In 1838, U.S. troops rounded up Cherokee families and held them in camps. People then traveled west on different routes, using roads, wagons, and waterways. The main forced journeys continued into 1839.

Illness, hunger, exposure, and harsh conditions killed thousands. The Trail of Tears refers to this devastating removal; it should not be pictured as one neat trail or one identical experience for every family.

Survivors rebuilt homes, institutions, and communities in Indian Territory. The Cherokee story includes forced departure and rebuilding, as well as the families who remained in or later returned to the eastern homeland.

Read the treaty story from more than one side

John Ross protested that the Treaty of New Echota did not represent the Cherokee Nation’s wishes. His protest matters because it shows that removal was opposed through political argument and written petitions as well as other forms of resistance.

If a few people sign a treaty, what else would you need to know?

Ask who authorized the signers, whether the nation’s government accepted the agreement, whether the people had a meaningful choice, and what pressure was used. A signature alone does not explain consent.

Explore New Echota

New Echota’s reconstructed and surviving buildings connect national government with printing and daily life. A print shop makes the newspaper’s production concrete; council and court spaces show that this was a political capital.

The site is also connected with the treaty used to justify removal. Visiting a place can reveal relationships that a list of dates misses: government, language, diplomacy, and dispossession happened in connected spaces.

Cherokee nations today

Three federally recognized Cherokee tribes are the Cherokee Nation and United Keetoowah Band of Cherokee Indians in Oklahoma, and the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians in North Carolina. The Eastern Band’s history includes families who remained in the east and others who returned.

Cherokee governments provide services and defend their nations’ interests. Language programs, schools, artists, families, and newspapers keep Cherokee voices active. The syllabary is still used, and the Cherokee Phoenix continues publication.

Cherokee Nation emphasizes that culture is always evolving and that practices differ among people and communities. Contemporary homes, careers, technology, and religious choices coexist with heritage.

Think first. Open each question to check your answer.

What did Sequoyah create?

A syllabary: a writing system whose characters represent syllables in Cherokee. He did not invent the spoken language.

Why did the Cherokee Phoenix matter?

It published in Cherokee and English, shared news, and helped express Cherokee views and defend national interests.

Did adopting a constitution and winning a court case prevent removal?

No. Most Cherokee people opposed the removal treaty, but the United States still carried out forced removal.
