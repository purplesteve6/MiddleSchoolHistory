Pueblo Peoples

Pueblo is not one tribe. It is a Spanish word meaning “town” or “village” that became a broad label for many distinct Native peoples whose communities were built around permanent villages in the Southwest.

Today, Pueblo nations include communities speaking Tewa, Tiwa, Towa, Keres, Zuni, and Hopi languages, each with its own identity, government, traditions, and history.

Pueblo peoples are many distinct nations whose permanent farming communities, ceremonies, and political traditions survived centuries of colonization and continue today.

Why the name “Pueblo”?

Spanish explorers used the word pueblo, meaning “town” or “village,” for the large permanent communities they encountered in the Southwest. Some contained multi-story blocks of rooms built from adobe or stone around plazas. Europeans then stretched the word from a kind of settlement into a broad label for the peoples who lived in them.

The shared label can be useful, but it can also hide important differences. Acoma, Taos, Zuni, Jemez, San Ildefonso, Ohkay Owingeh, Cochiti, and other Pueblo communities are distinct nations with their own governments, languages, migration histories, clans, ceremonies, and artistic traditions.

That is why this page uses Pueblo peoples in the plural. Similar architecture and farming patterns do not make many nations into one tribe.

Farming in a dry land

For many Pueblo communities, agriculture centered on corn, beans, and squash, supplemented by hunting and gathering. Farmers developed strategies for dry conditions, including careful use of runoff, floodplains, soil moisture, check dams, and irrigation where local geography allowed it.

Permanent villages made it possible to store harvests, organize communal work, specialize in pottery and other crafts, maintain plazas, and hold ceremonies. Thick adobe walls could help moderate temperature, while clustered rooms used land efficiently and brought families close together.

Adobe is a building material rather than a tribal identity. Pueblo builders mixed earth and water with plant fibers such as straw, forming walls that had to be maintained and replastered over time. Wooden roof beams called vigas supported flat roofs.

Kivas and ceremonial life

A kiva is a ceremonial and community space used by many Pueblo peoples. At ancestral sites, kivas were often circular and partly or completely below ground; in living communities, forms differ. Their exact meanings and uses belong to the traditions of particular pueblos.

Pueblo religion is living religion. Some dances and feast-day events may be open to visitors, while other ceremonies, rooms, objects, stories, and knowledge are private. Historians should not treat sacred traditions as secrets outsiders are entitled to uncover.

Cliff dwellings such as those at Mesa Verde are part of the history of Ancestral Pueblo peoples, whose descendants include modern Pueblo communities and other Native peoples of the Southwest. Those cliff villages were only one chapter in a much longer history of pithouses, mesa-top villages, pueblos, migration, and living communities.

One Pueblo tradition: Acoma

Searching for Haak'u

There is no single “Pueblo creation story,” because Pueblo peoples are many nations. One example comes from Acoma Pueblo. Acoma oral tradition reaches back to emergence and a long migration in search of a place that had been prepared for the people—a homeland called Haak'u.

The journey was not just about finding good real estate. The promised place was a spiritual homeland. Acoma tradition ties the people to the high mesa where the community stands today. The name is often explained as a “place that always was,” expressing a relationship between people and place much older than Spanish maps or written records.

Archaeology and oral tradition ask different kinds of questions and do not always give identical dates. Rather than forcing one to cancel out the other, historians can recognize that Acoma people use oral tradition to explain identity, belonging, responsibility, and continuity with Haak'u.

First encounters with Spain

Spanish expeditions entered the Pueblo world in the 1500s searching for wealth and routes of conquest. Coronado's expedition attacked the Zuni pueblo of Hawikuh in 1540 and later fought Tiguex communities along the Rio Grande. At Acoma, later encounters with Juan de Oñate's colony turned into brutal warfare in 1598–1599.

Permanent Spanish colonization placed missions, officials, soldiers, and settlers among Pueblo communities. Colonial authorities demanded labor and tribute and missionaries tried to replace Native religious practices with Catholicism. Disease and new economic demands added further pressure.

Pueblo communities did not all respond the same way. People traded, negotiated, adopted selected new goods or practices, protected traditions, fled missions, resisted colonial demands, and sometimes fought. Cooperation and resistance could happen within the same community at different moments.

Po'pay

Po'pay was a Tewa religious leader from Ohkay Owingeh who became a major organizer of the Pueblo Revolt of 1680. Runners carried instructions between communities, helping coordinate an uprising across a wide area.

The revolt drove Spanish authorities and settlers out of New Mexico for twelve years—one of the most successful Indigenous uprisings against European colonial rule in North American history.

The Pueblo Revolt of 1680

By 1680, years of religious persecution, forced labor, tribute, drought, hunger, and violence had created a crisis. Po'pay and other leaders built a remarkable network between Pueblo communities that spoke different languages and had their own local interests. Runners carried knotted cords to help coordinate the date of the uprising.

Pueblo peoples today

Pueblo communities remain sovereign Native nations. People continue languages, dances, ceremonies, farming traditions, pottery, weaving, government, education, and many other parts of community life. Some pueblos are internationally known for pottery or architecture; others place special emphasis on farming, language revitalization, or protection of sacred landscapes.

Modern Pueblo governments make decisions about water, land, tourism, economic development, education, and cultural preservation. The strongest way to understand Pueblo history is to see ancestral sites and living communities as part of the same continuing story—not as a civilization that “disappeared” when people migrated from places such as Mesa Verde or Chaco.
