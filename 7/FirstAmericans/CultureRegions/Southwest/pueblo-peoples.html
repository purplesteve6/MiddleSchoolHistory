Pueblo Peoples

Pueblo is not one tribe. It is a Spanish word meaning “town” or “village” that became a broad label for many distinct Native peoples whose communities were built around permanent villages in the Southwest.

Today, Pueblo nations include communities speaking Tewa, Tiwa, Towa, Keres, Zuni, and Hopi languages, each with its own identity, government, traditions, and history.

Pueblo peoples are many distinct nations whose permanent farming communities, ceremonies, and political traditions survived centuries of colonization and continue today.

Why the name “Pueblo”?

Spanish explorers used the word pueblo for the large permanent villages they encountered. Some communities contained multi-story blocks of rooms built from adobe or stone around plazas.

The shared label can be useful, but it can also hide differences. Acoma, Taos, Zuni, Jemez, San Ildefonso, Ohkay Owingeh, and other Pueblo communities are not interchangeable.

Farming in a dry land

For many Pueblo communities, agriculture centered on corn, beans, and squash, supplemented by hunting and gathering. Farmers developed strategies for dry conditions, including careful use of runoff, floodplains, soil moisture, and irrigation where possible.

Permanent villages supported craft specialization, trade, public gatherings, and ceremonial life.

Kivas and ceremonial life

A kiva is a ceremonial space used by many Pueblo peoples. Some are partly or completely below ground; others are not. Their exact meanings and uses vary by community.

Pueblo religion is living religion. Some ceremonies are public, while other knowledge is private. Historians should not treat sacred traditions as secrets that outsiders are entitled to uncover.

First encounters with Spain

Spanish expeditions entered the Pueblo world in the 1500s. Coronado's expedition attacked the Zuni pueblo of Hawikuh in 1540 and later fought Tiguex communities during the winter of 1540–1541. Permanent Spanish colonization intensified after Juan de Oñate entered New Mexico in 1598.

Spanish officials and missionaries demanded labor, tribute, and conversion to Catholicism. Pueblo communities responded in different ways—sometimes trading or cooperating, sometimes resisting, and sometimes doing both at different moments.

Po'pay

Po'pay was a Tewa religious leader from Ohkay Owingeh who became a major organizer of the Pueblo Revolt of 1680. Runners carried instructions between communities, helping coordinate an uprising across a wide area.

The revolt drove Spanish authorities and settlers out of New Mexico for twelve years—one of the most successful Indigenous uprisings against European colonial rule in North American history.

Bandelier National Monument

Bandelier protects ancestral Pueblo homes, farming landscapes, and archaeological sites on the Pajarito Plateau. NPS interpretation emphasizes that the people did not “vanish”; descendants live in Pueblo communities such as Cochiti and San Ildefonso today.

Pueblo peoples today

Pueblo communities remain sovereign Native nations. People continue languages, dances, ceremonies, farming traditions, pottery, weaving, government, education, and many other parts of community life.

The strongest way to understand Pueblo history is to see ancestral sites and living communities as part of the same continuing story—not as a civilization that disappeared.
