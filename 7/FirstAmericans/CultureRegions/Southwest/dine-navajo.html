Peoples of the Southwest

Diné (Navajo)

Diné means “the People” and is the name many Navajo people use for themselves. Diné homelands center on the Four Corners region of Arizona, New Mexico, Utah, and Colorado.

Over centuries, Diné communities combined farming, hunting, trade, herding, weaving, and family life into a culture strongly tied to land and balance.

The Diné endured forced removal during the Long Walk, returned to their homeland through the Treaty of 1868, and built the largest Native reservation in the United States.

Between four sacred mountains

Traditional Diné geography is organized around four sacred mountains surrounding the homeland. This landscape is more than a location; it is part of identity, stories, ceremony, and ideas about living in balance.

Families often lived in dispersed homesteads rather than large compact villages. The hogan became one of the best-known forms of Diné architecture.

Food, sheep, and weaving

Before Spanish livestock arrived, Diné communities farmed corn and other crops, hunted, and traded with neighboring peoples. Sheep became especially important after Spanish colonists introduced them to the Southwest.

Sheep

Sheep provided meat, wool, trade goods, and a dependable resource for families.

Weaving

Diné weavers transformed wool into blankets and rugs known for technical skill, design, and beauty.

Trade

Textiles, livestock, jewelry, food, and other goods connected Diné families with nearby communities and distant markets.

Spanish contact and change

Spanish records use a form of the word “Navajo” by the early 1600s. Early accounts describe Diné communities farming corn, hunting, and trading meat, hides, salt, and other goods with Pueblo neighbors.

Relationships with Spanish settlements changed over time. Trade and exchange existed alongside raiding, retaliation, warfare, and competition over land and resources. Spanish sheep, horses, and metal tools also became woven into Diné life in new ways.

The Long Walk

In 1864, the U.S. military forcibly removed thousands of Diné people from their homeland. Families were made to walk hundreds of miles east to Bosque Redondo at Fort Sumner, New Mexico.

Diné people call the place Hwéeldi. Hunger, disease, poor water, failed crops, and confinement made the reservation disastrous. Many died during the marches and captivity.

After four years, Diné leaders negotiated the Treaty of 1868, which allowed the people to return to a portion of their homeland.

Contributions and continuity

Diné weaving and silversmithing became influential art traditions. In the twentieth century, Navajo Code Talkers used the Navajo language to create a military communications code during World War II that enemy forces never broke.

But contributions are not limited to famous wartime stories. Diné people continue to shape art, government, ranching, education, law, language preservation, business, environmental stewardship, and public life.

Canyon de Chelly National Monument

Canyon de Chelly lies within Navajo Trust Land and is managed through a partnership between the Navajo Nation and the National Park Service. It preserves ancestral sites while also remaining home to Diné families today. The canyon is also closely connected to the history of the Long Walk.

The Navajo Nation today

The Navajo Nation is a sovereign government whose reservation spans parts of Arizona, New Mexico, and Utah. It is the largest reservation in the United States by land area.

Diné language, family relationships, sheep, weaving, ceremonies, and connections to the land remain important while Diné communities also face modern questions involving water, energy, education, health care, economic development, and self-government.
