Peoples of the Southwest

Diné (Navajo)

Diné means “the People” and is the name many Navajo people use for themselves. Diné homelands center on the Four Corners region of Arizona, New Mexico, Utah, and Colorado.

Over centuries, Diné communities combined farming, hunting, trade, herding, weaving, and family life into a culture strongly tied to land and balance.

The Diné endured forced removal during the Long Walk, returned to their homeland through the Treaty of 1868, and built the largest Native reservation in the United States.

Between four sacred mountains

Traditional Diné geography is organized around four sacred mountains surrounding the homeland, often called Dinétah. This landscape is more than a location on a map: mountains, rivers, directions, stories, and places are tied to identity and teachings about living in balance.

Families often lived in dispersed homesteads rather than large compact villages. The hogan became one of the best-known forms of Diné architecture. Traditional forms include forked-stick and rounded structures; later hogans were often six- or eight-sided. Logs or posts formed the frame, earth helped cover and insulate it, and the doorway traditionally faced east toward the rising sun.

A hogan is not simply an old-fashioned house. Diné educational materials describe it as a foundation for family life, values, and ceremony—a small world ordered around ideas of harmony and proper relationships.

A Diné teaching story

The Hero Twins and Spider Woman

Diné tradition tells of twin brothers born to Changing Woman: Monster Slayer and Born for Water. As the boys grew, dangerous beings still threatened the people. The twins wanted to find their father, the Sun, and gain what they needed to protect their community.

On their journey they discovered Spider Woman living in an underground home. She did not simply solve the problem for them. She warned them about dangers ahead, taught them how to prepare, and gave them help they would need on the difficult path to the Sun. Navajo Nation teaching materials use this part of the story to emphasize the importance of mentors, preparation, courage, and learning from those with greater experience.

The twins eventually gained the means to confront the monsters and make the world safer. The story is connected to larger Diné teachings about Changing Woman, the Holy People, kinship, and hózhó—living in beauty, balance, and right relationship. This page tells only a public outline; songs, prayers, and ceremonial knowledge connected to these traditions belong to Diné communities.

Food, sheep, and weaving

Before Spanish livestock arrived, Diné communities farmed corn, beans, squash, and other crops, hunted, gathered wild foods, and traded with Pueblo neighbors. Spanish colonization introduced sheep, goats, cattle, and horses. Diné families did not simply copy Spanish practices—they adapted livestock into their own economy and culture.

Sheep

Sheep provided meat, wool, trade goods, and a dependable resource for families.

Weaving

Diné weavers transformed wool into blankets and rugs known for technical skill, design, and beauty.

Trade

Textiles, livestock, jewelry, food, and other goods connected Diné families with nearby communities and distant markets.

Spanish contact and change

Spanish records use a form of the word “Navajo” by the early 1600s. Early accounts describe Diné communities farming corn, hunting, and trading meat, hides, salt, and other goods with Pueblo neighbors. Long before fixed modern borders, Diné families were part of a regional network of movement and exchange.

Relationships with Spanish settlements changed over time. Trade and intermarriage existed alongside raiding, retaliation, warfare, enslavement, and competition over land and water. Sheep transformed weaving and household wealth, while horses made travel and herding faster and connected Diné people to a wider mounted world across the Southwest and Plains.

Those changes did not erase older Diné traditions. New animals and technologies were fitted into an existing culture whose central ideas about family, land, sacred geography, and balance remained Diné.

The Long Walk

In 1864, after years of fighting and a U.S. military campaign that destroyed crops, orchards, homes, and livestock, thousands of Diné people were forced from their homeland. Families were made to walk hundreds of miles east to Bosque Redondo at Fort Sumner, New Mexico. The removals happened in groups over different routes, so the “Long Walk” was not one single march.

Diné people call the place Hwéeldi. Hunger, disease, poor water, failed crops, shortages of firewood, and confinement made the reservation disastrous. Families who had lived across a huge homeland were crowded into an unfamiliar place under military control. Many people died during the marches and the years of captivity.

After four years, Diné leaders negotiated the Treaty of 1868. Unlike many removal treaties in U.S. history, it allowed the Diné to return to a portion of their own homeland. Families rebuilt homes, farms, and herds, and the reservation later expanded. The return remains a central part of modern Diné historical memory.

Contributions and continuity

Diné weaving became one of the most recognized textile traditions in North America, developing through generations of experimentation with wool, dyes, pattern, and trade. Silversmiths also created distinctive jewelry traditions using silver, turquoise, and other materials. These arts are not frozen “traditional crafts”; Diné artists continue to innovate.

In the twentieth century, Navajo Code Talkers used the Navajo language to build a highly effective military communications code during World War II. Their service became nationally famous, but Diné contributions reach far beyond wartime history.

Diné people continue to shape tribal and federal law, education, ranching, language revitalization, environmental stewardship, energy policy, medicine, art, business, and public life across the Southwest.

Canyon de Chelly National Monument

Canyon de Chelly lies within Navajo Trust Land and is managed through a partnership between the Navajo Nation and the National Park Service. It preserves ancestral sites while also remaining home to Diné families today. The canyon is also closely connected to the history of the Long Walk.

The Navajo Nation today

The Navajo Nation is a sovereign government whose land spans parts of Arizona, New Mexico, and Utah. It is the largest Native reservation in the United States by land area, with communities spread across an area larger than several U.S. states.

Diné language, clan relationships, sheep, weaving, ceremonies, and connections to the land remain important. At the same time, communities confront modern questions involving water rights, energy development, uranium contamination, roads, health care, education, housing, language preservation, and economic opportunity. Sovereignty means that Diné people debate and make decisions about these issues through their own government and institutions.
