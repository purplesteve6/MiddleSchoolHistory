Apache Peoples

“Apache” is not the name of one single tribe. It is a broad label for several related Native peoples whose homelands stretched across parts of present-day Arizona, New Mexico, Texas, Colorado, Oklahoma, and northern Mexico.

Different Apache groups had their own territories, leaders, histories, and traditions, but many spoke related Southern Athabaskan languages and organized life around small local groups connected by family.

Apache history is a story of several related peoples who adapted through mobility, family networks, skilled use of the land, and resistance to outside control.

Where did Apache peoples live?

Apache homelands covered an enormous area of mountains, desert basins, grasslands, and river valleys. Groups included the Chiricahua, Mescalero, Jicarilla, Western Apache, Lipan, and Plains Apache.

Because resources were scattered, many Apache communities moved seasonally. Hunting, gathering, trade, and in some areas farming were combined in different ways depending on local conditions.

Homes built for mobility

Many Apache families used homes that could be built from local materials and repaired or replaced as people moved.

Wickiups

Dome-shaped shelters made from a frame of flexible poles covered with brush, grass, bark, or hides.

Tipis

Some Plains Apache groups used hide-covered tipis that could be taken apart and transported.

Seasonal movement

Mobility helped families follow game, gather plants, visit trading partners, and respond to changing conditions.

Food, work, and family

Apache subsistence varied by group. Chiricahua communities, for example, hunted large game, gathered wild plants, and practiced some agriculture. Family organization was strongly matrilineal in many Apache communities. After marriage, a man often lived near his wife's relatives.

Women commonly gathered plant foods, prepared meals, made baskets and clothing, and managed much of household life. Men often hunted and defended the community. These patterns were important, but they were not identical in every Apache group.

Contact changed the Southwest

Spanish colonization brought new animals, goods, weapons, diseases, and pressures into the region. Horses especially changed travel, hunting, raiding, and warfare. Relationships with Spanish and Mexican settlements shifted between trade, negotiation, raiding, and open conflict.

After the United States gained control of much of the Southwest in the mid-1800s, conflict intensified as settlers and the U.S. military moved into Apache homelands and tried to confine Native communities to reservations.

Cochise

Cochise was a Chokonen Chiricahua leader whose people lived around Apache Pass and the Dragoon Mountains. A breakdown in relations after the Bascom Affair of 1861 helped begin years of warfare between Cochise's followers and U.S. forces.

In 1872, Cochise negotiated a reservation in part of the Chiricahua homeland. He died two years later.

Geronimo and the end of the Apache Wars

Geronimo—also known as Goyakla—was a Bedonkohe Apache religious leader and warrior, not a hereditary chief. After years of conflict with Mexican and U.S. forces, he became one of the best-known figures of the Apache Wars.

In 1886, Geronimo and a small group surrendered. The Chiricahua, including Apache scouts who had served the U.S. Army, were sent far from their homeland as prisoners to Florida, Alabama, and later Oklahoma. Some were finally allowed to move to the Mescalero Reservation in New Mexico in 1913.

Fort Bowie National Historic Site

Fort Bowie in southeastern Arizona interprets Apache Pass, the Chiricahua Apache, Cochise, Geronimo, and the long conflict known as the Apache Wars. It is one of the clearest National Park Service sites for studying this history on the landscape where it happened.

Apache nations today

Apache people are not a people of the past. Federally recognized Apache nations and tribes continue today in Arizona, New Mexico, and Oklahoma, including the White Mountain Apache Tribe, San Carlos Apache Tribe, Mescalero Apache Tribe, Jicarilla Apache Nation, Tonto Apache Tribe, Yavapai-Apache Nation, and Apache Tribe of Oklahoma.

Communities continue to preserve languages, ceremonies, family traditions, arts, and sovereign governments while also participating fully in modern American life.
