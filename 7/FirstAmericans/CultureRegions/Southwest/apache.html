Peoples of the Southwest

Apache Peoples

“Apache” is not the name of one single tribe. It is a broad label for several related Native peoples whose homelands stretched across parts of present-day Arizona, New Mexico, Texas, Colorado, Oklahoma, and northern Mexico.

Different Apache groups had their own territories, leaders, histories, and traditions, but many spoke related Southern Athabaskan languages and organized life around small local groups connected by family.

Apache history is a story of several related peoples who adapted through mobility, family networks, skilled use of the land, and resistance to outside control.

Where did Apache peoples live?

Apache homelands covered an enormous area of mountains, desert basins, grasslands, river valleys, and high plains. Groups included the Chiricahua, Mescalero, Jicarilla, Western Apache, Lipan, and Plains Apache. These names describe related peoples, but they do not mean everyone lived the same way or followed the same leaders.

Local environments shaped everyday life. Some communities hunted bison or deer over wide territories; others relied heavily on mescal, seeds, nuts, roots, and other gathered foods; some practiced farming as part of a mixed food system. Seasonal movement was not wandering without purpose. Families knew springs, plant-gathering areas, hunting grounds, mountain passes, and safe camping places in remarkable detail.

Homes built for mobility

Many Apache families used homes that could be built from local materials and matched the amount of time a group expected to stay in one place. Among the Mescalero, a brush shelter called a wickiup was a common temporary dwelling; other Apache groups also used related forms. Plains Apache peoples could use hide-covered tipis. The important idea is not that Apache homes were “simple,” but that they were practical designs matched to movement and local conditions.

Wickiups

Dome-shaped shelters made from a frame of flexible poles covered with brush, grass, bark, or hides.

Tipis

Some Plains Apache groups used hide-covered tipis that could be taken apart and transported.

Seasonal movement

Mobility helped families follow game, gather plants, visit trading partners, and respond to changing conditions.

Food, work, and family

Apache subsistence varied by group and season. Hunting might provide deer, antelope, bison, rabbits, or other game, while gathered foods could include mescal hearts, seeds, nuts, berries, roots, and cactus fruits. Some Apache communities also planted crops. The result was a flexible food system that depended on detailed knowledge of when and where resources would be available.

Family networks organized much of everyday life. Many Apache communities were matrilineal, and after marriage a man often lived near his wife's relatives. Women were highly skilled gatherers, food processors, basket makers, hide workers, and household managers; men commonly hunted and took responsibility for defense. These patterns could vary by place and time, and both women and men held knowledge essential to survival.

A story from Mescalero Apache tradition

White Painted Woman and the two sons

Mescalero Apache tradition tells that White Mountain is a sacred place connected to the beginning of the people. There, White Painted Woman gave birth during a violent rainstorm while thunder and lightning filled the sky. Her sons were Child of Water and Killer of Enemies.

The world was dangerous. Giant monsters threatened human beings and feared White Painted Woman and her children. She raised the boys to be courageous and capable. When they became men, the two sons confronted the monsters and destroyed them, making the earth safer for people to live upon.

The story does more than explain a heroic past. In Mescalero tradition, White Painted Woman represents strength, long life, and admirable womanhood. Her story is connected to the coming-of-age ceremony for young women. Because Apache peoples are diverse, this is presented specifically as a Mescalero Apache tradition rather than a story every Apache group necessarily tells in the same way.

Contact changed the Southwest

Spanish colonization introduced new animals, metal tools, firearms, diseases, mission systems, and new kinds of conflict into Apache homelands. The horse became especially important. It allowed riders to travel farther and faster, move goods, hunt on the plains, and strike or escape over greater distances.

Horses did not create Apache mobility—Apache families had long moved seasonally before horses arrived—but they changed its scale. Trade, raiding, diplomacy, and warfare increasingly connected Apache groups with Pueblo communities, Spanish settlements, Mexico, Comanche power on the Plains, and later the United States.

After the United States gained control of much of the Southwest in the mid-1800s, conflict intensified as miners, ranchers, settlers, and the U.S. military entered Apache homelands. Federal policy increasingly tried to confine Apache communities to reservations, disrupting older movement patterns and access to land.

Cochise

Cochise was a Chokonen Chiricahua leader whose people lived around Apache Pass and the Dragoon Mountains. A breakdown in relations after the Bascom Affair of 1861 helped begin years of warfare between Cochise's followers and U.S. forces.

In 1872, Cochise negotiated a reservation in part of the Chiricahua homeland. He died two years later.

Geronimo and the end of the Apache Wars

Geronimo—also known as Goyakla—was a Bedonkohe Apache religious leader and warrior, not a hereditary chief. Mexican soldiers killed members of his family, and he later became known for repeated campaigns against Mexican and U.S. forces. His reputation grew far beyond the number of people actually traveling with him.

In 1886, Geronimo and a small group surrendered to General Nelson Miles. The surrender ended the last major armed Chiricahua resistance to the United States, but punishment extended far beyond Geronimo's followers. Chiricahua people—including Apache scouts who had served the U.S. Army—were sent far from their homeland as prisoners to Florida, Alabama, and later Oklahoma. Some were finally allowed to move to the Mescalero Reservation in New Mexico in 1913, while others remained in Oklahoma.

Fort Bowie National Historic Site

Fort Bowie in southeastern Arizona interprets Apache Pass, the Chiricahua Apache, Cochise, Geronimo, and the long conflict known as the Apache Wars. It is one of the clearest National Park Service sites for studying this history on the landscape where it happened.

Apache nations today

Apache people are not a people of the past. Federally recognized Apache nations and tribes continue today in Arizona, New Mexico, and Oklahoma, including the White Mountain Apache Tribe, San Carlos Apache Tribe, Mescalero Apache Tribe, Jicarilla Apache Nation, Tonto Apache Tribe, Yavapai-Apache Nation, Fort Sill Apache Tribe, and Apache Tribe of Oklahoma.

These nations run governments, schools, cultural programs, businesses, courts, and natural-resource programs. Communities continue to protect languages, ceremonies, family traditions, arts, and sacred places while participating fully in modern American life. Their histories did not end when the Apache Wars did.
