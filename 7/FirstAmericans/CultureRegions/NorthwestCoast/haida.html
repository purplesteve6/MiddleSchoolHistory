Peoples of the Northwest Coast

Haida

Haida history is inseparable from Haida Gwaii, an island homeland off the coast of British Columbia. Rich marine waters, cedar forests, and a network of villages supported expert seafarers, traders, carvers, and fishers. Haida communities also extend into Southeast Alaska. Haida people describe their culture through a close relationship with land, sea, ancestors, and future generations.

Haida Gwaii is not just where Haida history happened. It remains the center of Haida identity, governance, stewardship, language, art, and responsibility.

Islands of abundance—and skill

Haida Gwaii contains rainforests, rocky shores, bays, inlets, rivers, beaches, and a rich surrounding ocean. Salmon, halibut, herring, shellfish, seaweed, sea mammals, birds, and forest resources supported communities for thousands of years.

That abundance still demanded knowledge. Dangerous tides, reefs, storms, and exposed ocean crossings required generations of navigation and canoe-building skill.

Tsuuaay — cedar

A material tied to cultural survival

The Council of the Haida Nation describes cedar as essential to Haida well-being. Wood became ocean-going canoes, house posts, beams, bentwood boxes, masks, and monumental poles. Bark could be woven into clothing and baskets.

Clan, matrilineal identity, and monumental art

The Haida Nation identifies as a matrilineal society. Haida clans belong to Raven or Eagle moieties. Clan histories and rights can be expressed through names, crests, songs, stories, houses, regalia, and poles.

Haida artists are internationally famous for formline design, carving, jewelry, and monumental work. That fame can create a classroom trap: treating the art as decoration while ignoring who owns the histories represented.

A crest may identify inherited clan connections. A pole may memorialize people, represent clan history, or stand in relation to a house. The safest rule is to ask who made it, for whom, where, and why?

Contact, trade, and devastating disease

Recorded European contact began in the late 1700s. Maritime traders wanted sea-otter pelts, and Haida traders became major participants in the coastal trade. But introduced disease caused losses on a catastrophic scale.

Late 1700s
Maritime fur trade.
Foreign ships enter Haida waters and seek sea-otter pelts. Trade changes economies and warfare while commercial hunting also damages sea-otter populations.
1800s
Epidemics strike again and again.
Smallpox, measles, tuberculosis, and other diseases kill huge numbers of Haida people.
Late 1800s
Survivors gather into fewer communities.
Parks Canada estimates that fewer than 600 Haida remained on Haida Gwaii by the late nineteenth century after a population of at least 20,000 around first contact.

A value named by the Haida Nation

Yahguudang — respect

Parks Canada explains Yahguudang as a Haida idea of respect for all living things. That helps make sense of why land, ocean, cedar, salmon, ancestors, and future generations are often discussed together rather than as separate subjects.

Instead of treating this as a simple vocabulary word, use it as a question: what would “respect” require when deciding how much to harvest, how to manage a forest, or how to care for an ancestral village?

Gwaii Haanas

Gwaii Haanas National Park Reserve, National Marine Conservation Area Reserve, and Haida Heritage Site protects land and sea together. It is cooperatively managed by the Haida Nation and Canada. Haida Gwaii Watchmen care for village sites and help visitors understand that these places remain part of a living Haida world.

The Haida Nation today

The Council of the Haida Nation represents Haida citizens and works on governance, language, land, marine stewardship, and cultural protection. Old Massett and Skidegate remain major Haida communities on Haida Gwaii.

Modern Haida art, language work, fisheries programs, Watchmen, and land-management efforts all challenge the idea that monumental poles and ancestral villages belong only to the past. Cultural survival is happening in the present tense.

Public speaking, singing, dancing, carving, canoe work, and cultural gatherings all make the same point: Haida life is not frozen in a museum display. Communities continue to teach young people, protect the islands, and present Haida identity on Haida terms.
