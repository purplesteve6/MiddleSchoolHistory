Culture Region • Pacific Northwest Coast

Native Americans of the Pacific Northwest Coast

From the misty islands of Southeast Alaska to the river mouths and ocean beaches of Washington and Oregon, the Northwest Coast was shaped by water. Salmon returned to rivers in enormous runs. Cedar forests supplied building material. Protected inlets connected villages, while dangerous open-ocean waters rewarded expert canoe builders and navigators. The region supported large, permanent communities—but never one single “Northwest Coast culture.”

Abundant resources did not make every nation the same. Tlingit, Haida, Makah, Coast Salish, Chinookan, Tsimshian, Nuu-chah-nulth, Kwakwaka'wakw, Quileute, and many others developed their own languages, governments, histories, art, and traditions.

The Northwest Coast was a world of permanent villages connected by rivers and the sea. People moved seasonally to harvest resources, but that is very different from having no permanent home.

Where is the Northwest Coast?

Culture-region maps draw the boundaries differently, but the Northwest Coast generally follows the Pacific shoreline from Southeast Alaska through coastal British Columbia and into Washington and Oregon. Some classroom maps extend connections into the southern Yukon because Native homelands and trade networks never followed modern borders.

The landscape is unusually broken up: islands, fjords, bays, river mouths, peninsulas, rocky beaches, and rainforest sit side by side. That geography made waterways into roads.

Rivers

Salmon streams connected inland spawning grounds with coastal villages.

Islands & inlets

Sheltered waterways supported canoe travel, fishing, trade, and communication.

Rainforest

Western red cedar, spruce, hemlock, roots, bark, berries, and game supplied essential materials.

A world built around water

Why did salmon matter so much?

Salmon were food, but they were also tied to identity, ceremony, trade, responsibility, and the yearly rhythm of community life. Different nations developed specialized nets, traps, hooks, spears, weirs, drying methods, smokehouses, and fishing places. Many Native people of the region still describe themselves as Salmon People.

1Return

Adult salmon leave the ocean and swim back toward the rivers where they began life.

2Harvest

Families use deep knowledge of timing, currents, fish behavior, and specific fishing places.

3Preserve

Fish can be dried, smoked, roasted, or stored so a short run feeds people long after the season ends.

4Share

Salmon supports households, trade, feasts, ceremonies, and responsibilities between families.

5Protect

Today Native nations lead habitat restoration and fisheries work so salmon can keep returning.

First Salmon traditions

Across many Northwest cultures, the first salmon of the season is treated with special respect. Details differ by nation, but the larger lesson is consistent: a successful harvest creates responsibilities as well as benefits.

That relationship continues today. Pollution, dams, habitat loss, and changing water conditions can threaten salmon—and therefore threaten food, culture, and treaty-protected ways of life at the same time.

One tree, many technologies

Cedar shaped daily life

Western red cedar was extraordinarily useful because large straight trunks could be split into planks or hollowed into canoes, while bark and roots could become flexible fibers. Northern peoples also used spruce and other woods. Skilled harvesting could take bark or wood while leaving a living tree standing.

Plank housesLarge winter homes built from posts, beams, and removable cedar planks.
CanoesSpecialized vessels for ocean travel, rivers, freight, fishing, hunting, and ceremony.

FiberBark, roots, and other plant fibers became mats, baskets, rope, hats, and some clothing.

Permanent villages + seasonal movement

Older school materials sometimes call Northwest Coast people “nomadic.” That misses the most important part of the settlement pattern.

Winter villages

Large cedar plank houses stood in established villages. Houses could shelter extended families and display family or clan identity. Stored foods helped support people through the winter.

⇄

Seasonal camps

At other times of year, families traveled to fishing sites, berry grounds, hunting areas, shellfish beaches, or trading places. Temporary shelters and smaller camps put people close to resources when they were available.

Think of it as a home base plus a yearly resource map. People could be highly mobile during part of the year while still belonging to permanent villages with deep histories.

Canoes were not one-size-fits-all

Water travel required engineering. A canoe built to pole through a shallow river should not look exactly like one meant to cross exposed ocean swells.

Ocean-going canoes

Large dugouts could carry crews, cargo, hunters, or trading parties through rough coastal water.

River canoes

Shallow-water designs such as shovel-nose canoes favored agility and easy upriver travel.

Ceremonial canoes

Today canoe families and Tribal Canoe Journeys connect communities, languages, youth, and protocol across the coast.

Art as history, identity, and ownership

Poles and formline are not generic decorations

“Totem pole” is a common English term, but Northwest Coast peoples carved several kinds of monumental poles and house posts. Depending on the nation and purpose, they could record clan crests, family histories, memorialize people, mark a house, or witness important events. The figures are not a random stack of favorite animals.

⚡
A powerful being in Northwest Coast traditions

Thunderbird

Thunderbird appears in the traditions and art of several Northwest Coast peoples. It is a powerful supernatural bird associated with thunder, lightning, and great strength. In some traditions, Thunderbird is also closely connected with whales.

Important: there is no single universal “Thunderbird story.” Meanings differ among nations and communities, so a Thunderbird on a pole, drum, headdress, carving, or other artwork should be understood in its specific cultural context whenever possible.

In Southeast Alaska and northern British Columbia, Tlingit, Haida, and Tsimshian artists developed the visual system now often called formline. Click the shapes below to focus the explainer.

What is a potlatch?

A potlatch is not simply “a party where people give away presents.” Gift giving can be central, but these gatherings may also witness names, marriages, memorials, status, rights, songs, or family histories. Specific purposes and ceremonies vary by nation.

Language note: “Potlatch” comes through Chinook Jargon and is widely used in English. Individual nations also have their own words for particular ceremonies. For example, Sealaska Heritage uses the Tlingit term ku.éex’ for a memorial ceremony that English speakers have often called a potlatch.

Contact brought exchange—and catastrophe

Northwest Coast peoples had traded with one another for centuries before European and American ships entered the region. Newcomers joined existing trade networks, especially the maritime fur trade, but they also brought diseases, colonial governments, land loss, resource competition, missionary pressure, and efforts to suppress Native languages and ceremonies.

Late 1700s
Maritime trade expands.
European and American ships seek sea-otter pelts and other goods. Native nations bargain, travel, and reshape trade networks.
1800s
Epidemics devastate communities.
Smallpox and other introduced diseases kill enormous numbers of people in many coastal nations.
Mid–late 1800s
Colonial control deepens.
Treaties, reservations, extraction industries, schools, and new laws restrict Native access to land, fisheries, languages, and ceremonies.
1900s–today
Survival becomes revitalization.
Native nations strengthen language programs, carving, canoe traditions, fisheries, ceremonies, governance, and stewardship.

Shared coast ≠ shared identity

A Tlingit clan house in Southeast Alaska, a Haida village on Haida Gwaii, a Makah whaling crew at Cape Flattery, a Lummi reef-net fishery in the Salish Sea, and a Chinookan trading town on the lower Columbia all belong in the Northwest Coast story—but they are not interchangeable.
