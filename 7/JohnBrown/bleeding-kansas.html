1855–1856 • Kansas Territory

Bleeding Kansas

Before Harpers Ferry, John Brown became nationally known in Kansas, where proslavery and antislavery settlers were already fighting over the territory's future. The violence there convinced Brown that the struggle over slavery was moving beyond speeches and elections.

Kansas became a preview of the Civil War: Americans were already fighting and killing one another over slavery years before Fort Sumter.

Why Kansas Exploded

The Kansas-Nebraska Act of 1854 overturned the old expectation that slavery would be barred from much of this western territory. Instead, Congress used popular sovereignty, meaning the settlers themselves would vote on whether slavery would be legal.

That made control of Kansas extremely important. Antislavery settlers moved there hoping to create a free state, while proslavery settlers wanted slavery to expand westward. Armed men from neighboring Missouri—often called Border Ruffians—crossed the border to influence elections and intimidate opponents. Kansas soon had rival governments, disputed elections, armed militias, and outbreaks of political violence.

Brown traveled to Kansas in 1855 to join several of his sons, who were already part of the free-state movement. He brought weapons with him. By the spring of 1856, Brown no longer saw Kansas as simply a political contest; he saw it as an armed struggle over whether slavery would continue to spread.

How the Violence Escalated

On May 21, a large proslavery force entered Lawrence, one of the strongest antislavery towns in Kansas Territory. The attackers smashed the presses of two free-state newspapers, looted homes and businesses, and destroyed the Free State Hotel with cannon fire and flames. The attack caused relatively little loss of life, but the destruction sent a frightening message: political disagreements over slavery were now being settled with organized force. News of the Sack of Lawrence enraged antislavery settlers, including John Brown, who believed that simply waiting for legal protection would leave free-state communities defenseless.
Three nights after the attack on Lawrence, Brown led a small party that included several of his sons into settlements along Pottawatomie Creek. They took five proslavery men from their homes and killed them with broadswords. The victims were not soldiers killed during a battle, which made the killings especially controversial even among some people who opposed slavery. Brown's exact physical role in each death has long been debated, but he led the group and defended the action as retaliation against proslavery threats and violence. Pottawatomie greatly intensified the conflict and became one of the darkest and most debated episodes of Brown's life.
On June 2, Brown and a small free-state force confronted a proslavery group led by Henry Clay Pate near present-day Baldwin City. The two sides exchanged gunfire for several hours from positions around a wooded ravine and open prairie. Brown's men eventually forced Pate and many of his followers to surrender, taking them prisoner. Unlike the killings at Pottawatomie, Black Jack was a recognizable armed battle between organized groups fighting over the future of slavery in Kansas. Because Americans were already taking up weapons against one another over slavery, the Battle of Black Jack is sometimes described as an early preview of the Civil War.
By late August, the violence had grown into repeated raids and armed clashes. A much larger proslavery force moved against the free-state town of Osawatomie on August 30. Brown gathered a few dozen defenders and tried to slow the attackers from positions near the town, but his men were badly outnumbered and eventually had to retreat. Brown's son Frederick was killed as proslavery forces moved into the area, and much of Osawatomie was later burned. Although Brown did not win the battle, stories of his stubborn resistance spread widely and helped give him the nickname “Osawatomie Brown.”
The fighting at Black Jack has sometimes been described as one of the first battles of the coming Civil War because organized armed groups were already fighting over slavery in 1856.

The Pottawatomie Problem

Brown believed the attack on Lawrence proved that proslavery forces would continue using intimidation and violence unless antislavery settlers showed that they could strike back. Three nights later, he led a small party—including several of his sons—to settlements along Pottawatomie Creek.

The group took five proslavery men from their homes and killed them with broadswords. The victims had not been killed in battle and had not taken part in the Sack of Lawrence. Brown's exact physical role in each death has long been debated, but he led the party and later defended the action as part of the struggle against slavery.

The killings shocked people on both sides. Supporters argued that Brown was answering a larger campaign of threats and violence against free-state settlers. Critics—including some people who opposed slavery—called the killings murder. Pottawatomie therefore became one of the strongest pieces of evidence used both by Brown's defenders and by those who saw him as a dangerous extremist.

This is one reason Brown's legacy cannot be reduced to a simple hero-or-villain story.
