October 16–18, 1859

The Raid on Harpers Ferry

Brown planned a dramatic strike against slavery: seize the federal armory and arsenal, capture government weapons, arm an expanding liberation force, and use the nearby mountains as a base for future action. He hoped the raid would help start a widespread uprising against slavery, with enslaved people escaping, joining his force, and helping others fight for freedom.

The raid failed militarily in only about 36 hours—but its political impact lasted far longer.

The Plan

Why Harpers Ferry?

Harpers Ferry was a small Virginia town in 1859, located where the Potomac and Shenandoah Rivers met. During the Civil War, West Virginia separated from Virginia and became a state in 1863; Harpers Ferry is in West Virginia today. The town had enormous military importance because the federal government operated a major U.S. armory and arsenal there, along with a rifle works. Brown hoped to seize government weapons and move them into the nearby mountains.

From there, he imagined creating a chain of mountain strongholds. Enslaved people would escape to his force, receive weapons or pikes, and help lead more people away from plantations. Brown did not expect to conquer the entire South in one battle; he hoped slavery would become increasingly difficult to maintain as the movement grew.

Kennedy Farm

Brown rented a Maryland farmhouse about five miles from Harpers Ferry under the false name “Isaac Smith.” During the summer of 1859, his followers quietly gathered there. Twenty-one men eventually joined the operation, and they often hid in the attic during the day so neighbors would not become suspicious.

The farm became their secret headquarters. Brown stockpiled guns and hundreds of pikes, studied local maps, trained the men, wrote letters, and waited for the moment to strike. The secrecy worked: most people around Harpers Ferry had no idea an armed raid was being prepared nearby.

36 Hours at Harpers Ferry

Brown and 18 men leave Kennedy Farm and move toward Harpers Ferry. They cut telegraph lines, secure bridges, and begin taking control of key federal buildings. Other members of the group remain behind in support roles.
Raiders take the bridges, U.S. Armory and Arsenal, and the Rifle Works. They also take hostages, including Lewis Washington, a local slaveholder and relative of George Washington. The opening phase succeeds with surprisingly little resistance.
A Baltimore & Ohio train is stopped. Railroad worker Heyward Shepherd, a free Black man, is shot and mortally wounded. Brown eventually allows the train to continue, and news of the raid rapidly spreads beyond Harpers Ferry.
Armed townspeople and militia begin firing on the raiders and block important escape routes. Brown's group becomes trapped inside the town rather than moving into the mountains as planned. Several raiders are killed, including Dangerfield Newby.
U.S. Marines commanded by Army Colonel Robert E. Lee arrive. Lieutenant J.E.B. Stuart delivers a demand for surrender to Brown inside the fire engine house. Brown refuses. Marines smash through the doors, overpower the remaining raiders, and capture Brown alive.

Who Joined Brown?

Brown's force was interracial at a time when even many antislavery organizations were segregated. The group included white abolitionists, free Black men, and formerly enslaved men. Twenty-one men gathered for the operation, although only 19 entered Harpers Ferry with Brown that night. Their reasons for joining were personal as well as political: some wanted to strike slavery as a system, while others hoped the raid could help free family members and people they knew.

Brown's last stand took place in the Armory's small fire engine house. The building later became known as John Brown's Fort.

Why Did It Fail?

Too SlowBrown did not immediately leave town with weapons. The longer he stayed, the more time local forces had to organize.
Local ResistanceTownspeople and militia quickly blocked bridges and roads, cutting the raiders off from the mountain escape Brown had imagined.
No Mass UprisingThe large wave of enslaved recruits Brown hoped would join him did not appear during the raid's short and chaotic life.

Brown also took hostages and became reluctant to abandon some of them, which slowed his movement further. By the morning of October 18, the raid was over. Ten of Brown's men had been killed, five eventually escaped, and Brown and several others were captured.

Militarily, Harpers Ferry was a clear failure. Politically, however, the raid was just beginning to have an effect. Newspapers spread the story across the country, white Southerners feared that more abolitionist attacks might follow, and Brown suddenly became a national symbol.
