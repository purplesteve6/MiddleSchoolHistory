First Bull Run / Manassas, Virginia, southwest of Washington, D.C.

First Bull Run showed both sides that the Civil War would not be quick or easy.

Before the Fighting

The “quick war” illusion

In the summer of 1861, many Northerners believed one hard blow would crush the rebellion. Union General Irvin McDowell marched out from Washington toward Manassas Junction, an important railroad center in Virginia. The plan was ambitious for an army made up largely of inexperienced volunteers whose training and organization were still weak.

What both sides thought victory would mean

Many Northerners thought a major Union win could crush the rebellion quickly and open the way toward Richmond. Many Confederates hoped another major victory would convince the North to give up and leave the new Confederate States alone.

Why Manassas mattered

The Confederates did not rely on one army alone. Troops under Joseph E. Johnston used the railroad from the Shenandoah Valley to reinforce Beauregard, showing how important transportation would be in the war. The battlefield also sat close enough to Washington that politicians and even curious civilians treated the campaign almost like a public spectacle—until the reality of battle shattered that idea.

Why both armies had problems

Both armies were inexperienced. McDowell’s Union plan was too complicated for many new soldiers to carry out smoothly, while Confederate units struggled with communication and coordination. Confusion on both sides made the battle difficult to control.

Thomas “Stonewall” Jackson

Jackson became famous at Bull Run. His stubborn stand on Henry House Hill inspired the nickname “Stonewall,” one of the most memorable names of the war.

How It Unfolded

1

The Union attack begins well

Union troops crossed Bull Run and hit the Confederate left. For part of the day, it looked as if McDowell might break the Confederate line and claim the early victory the North wanted.

2

Confederates rally on the high ground

On Henry House Hill, Confederate troops stiffened their line. Jackson’s brigade helped hold the position while additional Southern forces reached the field and stabilized the defense.

3

A retreat becomes a rout

Late in the day, Union units broke and streamed back toward Washington. Civilians and wagons clogged the roads. The Confederates won, though they were too disorganized to destroy the retreating Union army.

Some civilians came from Washington with picnic baskets to watch the battle, expecting an easy Union victory. Instead, they ended up caught in the panic of the retreat.

Politics & the War

Why this mattered beyond the battlefield

Bull Run convinced political leaders that the war would not end in a single summer campaign. It helped bring George B. McClellan to prominence, because Northern leaders now wanted someone to reorganize the Army of the Potomac into a stronger, more professional force.

The most important result of Bull Run was not simply who won one day. It was the collapse of the fantasy that the war would be short and simple.

Why It Mattered

First Bull Run forced both sides to rethink the war. The North needed bigger, better-organized armies, while the South gained confidence that it could resist. More than anything, the battle destroyed the idea that one major clash would quickly end the war.
